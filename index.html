Session 1 — The Future of Work & Talent

3 items

Why talent management became a board-level question: the three tectonic forces reshaping work, a validated model for spotting leadership potential beyond past performance, and Bain's 2026 read on the macro forces rewriting the CPO's mandate.

Article Session 1

Talent Management and the Future of Work

Harvard Business School Note 9‑820‑084 · William Kerr & Gorick Ng · February 14, 2020

Names three tectonic forces — technology, demographics and globalisation — reshaping how firms access workers, and structures the whole problem as Forces of Change → Workplace Challenges → Managerial Levers, with fifteen specific managerial responses spanning talent management, workplace design and external collaboration.

Full digest: all three forces (15 sub-trends), three workplace challenges, and all 15 managerial levers

Core argument

The nature of work is changing rapidly, driven by three simultaneous, interacting macro-forces — likened by the authors to "tectonic plates rubbing against each other": technology advancement, demographic transition, and global integration. These forces are reshaping firms' ability to access the right workers with the right skills and are redefining worker productivity. The note's practical purpose is to give managers a structured framework — forces of change → workplace challenges → managerial levers — to diagnose what's happening in their industry and decide how to adapt. The opening hook: Amazon and AT&T are each investing billions of dollars to retrain roughly half their workforces for "jobs of the future."

Force 1 — Technology advancement

Illustrative comparison: Apple's 2018 iPhone had 100,000 times the processing power of the room-sized computers NASA used for the Apollo program in the 1960s, and was 3,300 times cheaper. Five sub-trends (cause → effect): (1) digitisation of nearly every industry/function → need to transform business models and legacy practices; (2) declining barriers to entry for technology-first players with different cost structures → need to rethink the value chain and value proposition; (3) pressure for labour to be automated or augmented → need to reevaluate how work gets done and how technique is taught; (4) unlocking of unprecedented latent data/insights → need for analytical methods and digitally savvy workers; (5) declining half-life of worker skills → need to continually keep skills sharp and relevant.

Tim Buckley (CEO, The Vanguard Group): "Technology will change the advice industry, and that's uncomfortable. You can fight it, you can deny it, or you can embrace it as an opportunity to scale your business better and lower your cost structure."

Force 2 — Demographic transition

Female labour-force entry has "mostly run its course"; birth rates peaked in the 1950s and have plummeted, pushing retirements to exceed new entrants. Millennials comprise 35% of the workforce — the largest generational cohort ever — and bring new preferences about work. Educated workers have concentrated in urban centres. Five sub-trends: (1) slower growth/decline in national workforces → need to identify and compete for scarcer, often "hidden," workers; (2) rising age and impending retirements → need to succession-plan and support caregiving burdens; (3) growing diversity of age/ethnicity/background → need to appreciate different worker needs; (4) changing attitudes of Millennials/Gen Z → need to rethink the firm-worker relationship and employer value proposition; (5) growing concentration of skilled workers in large cities → need to bridge concentrated talent pools with the broader workforce.

Greg Case (CEO, Aon Corporation): "People allocation is as powerful as financial allocation."

Force 3 — Global integration

Technology and globalisation have reinforced each other since WWII. Five sub-trends: (1) access to a global customer/client base → need to build overseas capabilities and local sensitivity; (2) access to an international talent pool → need to navigate immigration law and manage distributed workforces; (3) potential for work to be done anytime/anywhere → need to redefine productivity and manage remote/complex team structures; (4) continued pressure to place routine work in lower-cost settings → need to manage regulatory/reputational/operational risk; (5) emergence of formidable global competitors → need to safeguard IP and monitor foreign rivals. The note flags globalisation's downside — inequality fuelling populism/protectionism, citing Brexit.

Jamie Dimon (CEO, JPMorgan Chase): "Globalization — I don't think it has to be reversed… there were flaws… people were hurt by it… they went from earning $40 an hour to $10 an hour. We need to recognize that and do something about it… retraining, relocation, business redevelopment — all these things can work if properly done."

Workplace challenge 1 — Worker access

"Do we have access to sufficient quantities of the right talent?" Four access channels: (A) campus recruiting (internships, apprenticeships) — struggles: high cost competing against better-branded firms, inexperience requiring investment, churn risk, culture misalignment with younger workers; (B) active/passive job seekers (postings, recruiters, referrals) — struggles: poor/biased fit proxies, reliance on brand recognition, wage premiums, risk of low diversity from hiring people like existing staff; (C) "hidden workers" outside the labour force — explicitly named: the previously incarcerated, those recovering from substance abuse, the physically impaired, and parental-leave/retirement returners — struggles: reimagining hiring funnels, training investment for employment gaps, restructuring norms, addressing stigma; (D) internal talent reallocation to better-fitting roles, enabled by gig-matching algorithms — struggles: mapping role-specific skills, institutional inertia/funding constraints, friction from non-traditional career paths.

Workplace challenge 2 — Competencies and skills

Introduces "degree inflation" — job postings requiring more education than current workers actually possess — which inflates labour costs and shrinks the qualified pool. (A) Soft skills gap: as automation handles rule-based tasks, demand grows for teamwork/complex problem-solving (citing Autor, Levy & Murnane, 2003). Named examples: Walmart uses VR headsets for customer-service training; McDonald's elevates hiring of retirees for reliability/professionalism. Struggles: identifying/teaching what separates effective from mediocre workers; proving ROI on soft skills; confronting that soft skills reflect "cultural capital" not shared by all in diverse workplaces. (B) Digital skills gap: a John Deere tractor now has 30–40 on-board computers, requiring digital soft skills (customer-centricity, data-driven decisions, comfort with ambiguity) alongside technical literacy. Struggles: managing non-digital natives, funding upskilling knowing not everyone reaches proficiency, costly/disruptive transformations causing resistance and job cuts.

Workplace challenge 3 — Productivity at work

"Is this talent productive?" (A) Inputs → outputs: shift from the 8-hour/5-day, face-time model toward output/value-creation measurement — struggles: culture without collocation, misread non-verbal cues, assessing non-atomizable/non-quantifiable output. (B) Low- → high-value work: robotic process automation (insurance-claims processing, chatbots) frees workers for higher-value tasks — struggles: securing buy-in, risky bets on emergent tech, maintaining service quality. (C) Treatment → prevention: preventive health coaches/apps, tuition reimbursement/loan repayment for retention — struggles: engaging the highest-risk employees, securing leadership commitment, assessing ROI.

The 15 managerial levers (5 per category, framed as diagnostic questions)

Talent management: (1) assess/continuously train hard and soft skills; (2) capture and codify institutional memory for high-attrition roles; (3) mitigate obstacles preventing workers from considering certain roles; (4) mitigate obstacles preventing workers from being considered (separate skill signal from noise like gender/credentials); (5) systematically analyse/mitigate drivers of absenteeism and turnover.

Workplace design: (1) combat unspoken rules favouring "inputs" over "outputs"; (2) optimise the monetary/non-monetary employer value proposition; (3) assess how data/tech can improve productivity; (4) explore work-life balance policies (remote, parental, medical leave); (5) expand ways for workers to move between roles/functions.

External collaborations: (1) curriculum design/hiring partnerships with educational institutions; (2) partnerships with organisations serving non-traditional talent pools; (3) industry consortia to pilot/scale workforce development jointly; (4) engagement with lawmakers on worker availability/quality legislation; (5) systems to monitor/manage outsourcing and offshoring risk.

Practical implications

Managers must systematically diagnose which forces hit their business hardest, work through the three workplace-challenge questions, then select from the fifteen levers — an explicitly modular, diagnostic toolkit rather than a prescriptive solution.

Article Session 1

How to Spot — and Develop — High‑Potential Talent in Your Organization

Harvard Business Review · James Intagliata, Jennifer Sturman & Stephen Kincaid (ghSMART) · May 26, 2022

Past performance is a biased, incomplete proxy for future potential. Drawing on 23,000+ candidate assessments and an in-depth analysis of 1,500 individuals, ghSMART isolates three behavioural markers — Cognitive Quotient, Drive Quotient and Emotional Quotient — that predict leadership growth two times out of three, beating the roughly 50/50 odds of typical promotion decisions.

Full digest: the CQ/DQ/EQ model, both validation studies, development tactics, and the full "Maya" case

Core argument and methodology

Organisations traditionally identify future leaders by track record — who's taken on more responsibility, who's hit targets. This fails for two reasons: it can't predict who can do things they haven't done before, and it under-identifies high potentials early in career, especially those without equitable access to mentoring, sponsorship and advancement. The model draws on a database of 23,000+ candidate assessments, with in-depth analysis of 1,500 individuals ranging from entry-level professionals to senior leaders, to isolate three markers of how people use intellect, motivation and interpersonal style on the job — not raw measures of them.

Cognitive Quotient (CQ) — how they leverage their intellect

Critiques standard measures (transcripts, psychometric tests, case interviews) as biased toward elite schools and "hoop-jumping" over practical/commercial instinct. High-CQ behaviours: routinely stepping back to see things from a manager's (or manager's manager's) perspective; "looking around corners" to anticipate the unexpected; grounding even small decisions in how they create business value.

Drive Quotient (DQ) — what motivates them and how they apply energy

Distinguished from generic motivation/work ethic (common among aspiring leaders, non-differentiating). The true differentiator: applying energy not just to maximise own performance but to develop and leverage others' capabilities. High-DQ people push past comfort zones with relish, are resilient (reset/reframe after setbacks), and strive to amplify results at the organisational level.

Emotional Quotient (EQ) — how they interact with those around them

Goes beyond basic self-awareness/rapport (necessary but not sufficient) to "engaging for impact" — intentionally channelling insight to influence stakeholders and negotiate outcomes — and the courage and empathy to deliver difficult messages.

Validation study 1 — double-blind, early-career

Looking only at leaders' earlier career phases, the three markers differentiated future C-suite executives from those who didn't make it two times out of three (~67% accuracy) — significantly outperforming the roughly 50/50 odds of typical hiring/promotion decisions.

Validation study 2 — multi-year, 1,800-candidate, single organisation

Managers rated candidates' performance independently and blind to the researchers' potential ratings. Candidates rated highly on potential were more than three times as likely to be rated top performers in year one — a gap persisting through years two to four — and were less likely to be involuntarily terminated. Conclusion: the model captures a style of thinking and working that sets the stage for ongoing growth, not just current skill.

Development tactics by quotient

  • CQ: attend meetings with senior leaders for broader perspective; assignments requiring engagement across other parts of the organisation; participation in industry conferences/events for external exposure.
  • DQ: stretch opportunities — rotating into different markets/business areas, bigger teams — observing whether they proactively seek feedback rather than wait to be told.
  • EQ: spell out the organisation's "unwritten rules" of engagement; task with stakeholder mapping as an explicit development objective; introduce frameworks for understanding how they and others are personally "wired."

Practical rollout: educate managers that performance alone isn't a potential proxy, especially for candidates without a track record or matching prior leader "molds"; build formal "potential profiles" into performance management and development processes.

Case study: "Maya" (name changed)

A Fortune 500 client engaged ghSMART to identify/develop high potentials, mostly leaders two levels below the C-suite. Maya was three levels down, included only when a program spot unexpectedly opened — largely unknown to senior leaders, and those who did know her called her "too young," "too eager to please," lacking "gravitas."

CQ: impressed evaluators by weighing multiple scenarios rather than locking onto one answer, factoring in broader industry/competitive context — rated highly. DQ: immigrant parents in blue-collar jobs; chose a college for its financial package over prestige; sought stretch assignments from her earliest roles; volunteered to lead the firm's women's mentorship initiative. EQ (weakest area): her drive to deliver results came at the cost of relationship building; she struggled where persuasion (not just facts) was needed and avoided conflict rather than having tough conversations.

Coaching intervention: became more intentional about stakeholder engagement and how she "showed up"; held accountable for tackling difficult issues head-on; recommended for a cross-functional rotation. Outcome: within 24 months she jumped two levels into a high-visibility role where she is thriving — her new boss calls her a "rock star" — and she is now a leading C-suite feeder candidate.

Closing caveat

Not all behaviours are equally coachable — influence/persuasion skills are readily coachable, while thinking more conceptually/strategically may be harder to change. Managers should weigh how readily any missing behaviour can realistically be learned when assessing readiness for a new leadership role.

Article Session 1

The Great Talent Recalibration: How Macroeconomic Shifts Are Reshaping the CHRO Agenda

Bain & Company Insights · Karen Harris, Susan Gunn & John Hazan · January 2026

Insights from Bain's CPO Forum (81 surveyed CPOs) identify four structural forces — post-globalisation, capital scarcity, demographic decline and the falling cost of distance — pushing the CPO's mandate from "people steward" to "architect of the 2040 workforce," in what the authors call the most turbulent macro environment since the early 1970s.

Full digest: the 81-CPO survey, all four macro forces, the AI/human-interface question, and the CPO's evolving mandate

Core argument

"The context in which most HR leaders built their careers is not the context in which they will lead the future." Organisations are navigating the most turbulent macroeconomic environment in decades — unlike anything since the early 1970s — as post-globalisation impacts evolve, capital becomes constrained, labour pools shrink, and AI transforms work. The CPO's mandate is expanding from steward of people to architect of the 2040 workforce, spanning automation, location strategy and long-term skills access.

Survey data

Drawn from Bain's survey of 81 CPOs and talent officers across global industries. 70% reported concern about slowing US labour-market momentum spilling into EMEA and Asia-Pacific. A majority are considering hiring adjustments in select markets, and some are actively rebalancing their global hiring footprint. CPOs are prioritising HR efficiency, AI implementation, and organisational redesign alongside cultural/leadership needs — reflecting tension between investing in people versus automation.

Force 1 — Post-globalisation and new limits on talent mobility

Bain does not believe in full "deglobalisation," but a "post-global world" of growing friction in trade of goods, services, IP and capital — undermining the foundational assumption that talent can move freely across borders. Named example: in the semiconductor industry, the US has restricted not just where goods can be sold but which passport holders may work on specific technologies destined for certain markets — geopolitics directly shaping talent deployment. Takeaway: CPOs must plan for a world where geopolitical boundaries increasingly shape access to skills.

Force 2 — Capital scarcity and rising pressure on productivity

Marks the end of "capital superabundance" — for three decades the financial economy grew far faster than the real economy, making capital a lesser constraint than ideas or talent; this is reversing. Competing demands for capital (automation, energy transition, infrastructure) collectively exceed annual economic savings, and capital supply is expected to grow much more slowly than before — creating growing tension between investing in people versus automation and sharper scrutiny of ROI.

Force 3 — Demographic decline and structural labour shortages

Called "perhaps the most profound and least reversible force shaping the future workforce." Working-age populations are shrinking in China, Germany and Japan. Even in the US — assuming some mitigation of current immigration policy — total workforce is expected to grow only 4% between now and 2050. China, a country of over a billion people, recorded only 9.5 million births in 2024. India is identified as the only large economy with a workforce still growing at scale. Implication: companies cannot meet talent needs through hiring alone — automation becomes a productivity necessity, not just a cost lever.

Force 4 — The decreasing cost of distance

Technology has enabled greater spatial dispersion of work. Remote work surged during Covid-19 and has since stabilised near 20%; office occupancy remains at roughly 50% of pre-Covid levels. Hybrid work expands talent access but challenges apprenticeship, cohesion and culture, and physical infrastructure built for a previous era must adapt.

Automation, AI, and the human-machine frontier

Automation/AI form "a powerful throughline connecting demographics, capital, and globalisation" — generative AI and dexterity robotics push automation into sectors once thought resistant, like healthcare and food preparation. The central strategic question: "When do we need a human?" AI can be technically correct yet fail at the "human interface" — the common experience of trying to bypass a chatbot on a service call. Strategic implication: design workforces not to automate indiscriminately, but to determine where human presence — empathy, judgment, trust, nuance — matters most.

Geopolitics and the shifting map of global talent

Recent changes to US working visas are creating anxiety for employees and new burdens for employers, potentially eroding the US's status as the default destination for talent. Named example: AI translation tools let firms draw from new labour pools — e.g., hiring customer-support talent in Guatemala (time-zone aligned with the US) rather than the Philippines, as language barriers diminish.

The CPO role: from people steward to architect of the 2040 workforce

CPOs are now expected to help answer: In 2040, what is our workforce composition? Where do we have people versus automation? How do we get closest to talent? The role evolves from "steward of people" to "strategist of the entire workforce system" — automation strategy, location strategy, and long-term skills access, on a 2040 planning horizon.

Practical implications for CHROs/CPOs

  • Treat talent mobility as geopolitically constrained — build contingency plans around visa/trade shifts (semiconductor sector as cautionary template).
  • Expect capital to be scarcer and more contested — justify people investment with sharper ROI discipline against automation/energy/infrastructure competition.
  • Treat demographic decline as structural and largely irreversible — embrace automation as a productivity necessity in shrinking-workforce markets while watching India as the growth exception.
  • Reconfigure real estate, culture and apprenticeship models for a stabilised hybrid reality (~20% remote, ~50% office occupancy).
  • Use AI strategically — deliberately decide where humans remain essential rather than automating indiscriminately.
  • Rethink global talent-hub location strategy given immigration volatility and AI-enabled new labour pools.
  • Reframe the CPO/CHRO mandate from administering people programs to architecting the entire future workforce system.

Case Session 2

Somany Ceramics: Recruitment Challenges

Ivey Publishing, W18499 / 9B18C025 · Kodwani, Kumar & Prashar · 2018

In December 2016, SCL's HR director Hrishikesh Kumar leaves a meeting with the company's top executives both excited and troubled. India's ceramic-tile market is projected to grow from 12.3 to 21.8 billion m² of demand by 2020, and Joint MD Abhishek Somany wants SCL to capture the largest possible share of that growth — but the entire Indian ceramics industry suffers a structural shortage of technically trained labour, and engineering graduates overwhelmingly prefer IT (63% in one survey) over ceramics, drawn by nearly double the entry-level salary. Kumar must decide whether to "tug" trained workers away from rivals at a wage premium, or "train" inexperienced recruits from scratch on full salary — a decision his own team is split on.

Full brief: company & industry background, financials, competitors, the complete "tug vs. train" analysis, stakeholders, and discussion questions

Opening frame

The case opens in December 2016. Hrishikesh Kumar, Director of HR at Somany Ceramics Limited (SCL), has just met the top executives and is simultaneously excited (about industry growth prospects) and troubled (about how to staff for that growth). The core tension: the ceramic-tile market is projected to grow at a CAGR of 8.5%, from demand of 12.3 billion m² to 21.8 billion m² by 2020. Management wants Kumar to scale up labour to match this growth, but the entire ceramic-tile industry suffers an acute shortage of industry-ready technical labour. The question posed to students: how could Kumar hire and sustain a workforce of trained labourers over the next five years, sustainably and cost-effectively?

Company history and leadership

1969: founded as Somany Pilkington, a joint venture with Pilkington Tiles (UK). 1994: Indian promoters bought out all UK partner equity. 2007: renamed Somany Ceramics Limited (SCL). Three generations of Somany family leadership by 2016: H. L. Somany (founder, Chairman Emeritus), Shreekant Somany (Chairman & Managing Director), and Abhishek Somany (Joint Managing Director) — credited with driving consistent growth since 1969 and wanting the company to grow faster, requiring competent talent with technical ceramics expertise. Company vision: "the most sought-after tile and allied product [company] in India and the best employer in the tile industry." Headquartered in Noida, Uttar Pradesh.

Products, plants and capacity

Product range: floor tiles, wall tiles & claddings, chrome-plated fittings & accessories, sanitaryware (basins, cisterns, toilets, urinals), and tile-laying solutions. Manufacturing plants at Kassar (Haryana) and Kadi (Gujarat); combined annual capacity across plants and joint ventures of 41 million m². In 2015–16, Kassar capacity rose from 21.55 to 25.55 million m²/year, and subsidiary/associate capacity rose from 20.97 to 25.70 million m²/year. Distribution network: 1,490 dealers, 8,000 sub-dealers, 146 retail showrooms. Milestones: first in India to produce 45×45 cm tiles domestically (1996); ISO 9001 accreditation (1999); ISO 14001 for the Kadi facility. 2016 market capitalisation ≈ ₹31.02 billion (US$462.98 million).

Industry data — global and Indian ceramic-tile market

Global market valued at $76.81 billion (2015), forecast to reach $178.1 billion by 2024 (implied global CAGR ≈9.8%). Indian market estimated at ₹270 billion (~$4.03 billion) as of March 2016. Per-capita tile consumption in India: only 0.59 m² — far below Brazil (4.12 m²), China (3.33 m²), Vietnam (2.8 m²) — signalling large headroom. Growth drivers: Swachh Bharat Abhiyan, smart cities, urbanisation. Forecast Indian CAGR: 12–15%, faster than the 9.8% global rate. 45–50% of the Indian market remained fragmented among unbranded players. Sector employment: over 550,000 people (50,000 direct, 500,000 indirect).

Competitive landscape

Kajaria Ceramics (incorporated 1985) — largest Indian tile manufacturer, 1,200+ product options, exports to 20 countries, clients include DLF, Unitech, Sobha, Prestige Group; won Superbrands India 7 times by 2016; FY2015–16 net sales ₹24.48 billion (US$365.37M). HSIL Ltd. — founded by the Somany family itself in 1960 as Hindustan Twyfords; renamed HSIL in 2009; entered tiles properly via the 2010 Hindware Italian collection launch; 2016 net sales ₹19.44 billion (US$290.19M). Cera Sanitaryware — started 1980, tied up with Italy's Rondine Group; tiles only 12% of FY2014–15 turnover vs. ~60% from sanitaryware; 2016 revenue ₹9.34 billion (US$139.35M), up 13.63% YoY.

The labour-market problem — the "tug vs. train" dilemma

Roughly 780,000 engineers enter India's job market annually (150,000 of them IT engineers), but virtually all engineering graduates gravitate to IT for its glamour and benefits. A 2012 Nielsen Campus Track survey found 63% of 2013 graduates preferred IT services/products. IT firms would hire graduates of any specialisation and invest 3 months to a year training them — a scale ceramics firms could not match. Only 7–10% of new engineering graduates nationally were estimated employable for core engineering work at all.

Ceramics paid roughly half of IT entry salaries and well below construction — a structural disadvantage compounded by IT's perceived superiority on career opportunities, work environment, lifestyle, status and flexible schedules. Ceramics was seen as an "unconventional" career choice, and being more labour-intensive, needed far more workers per unit of output growth than IT or construction.

Option 1 — "Tug" (poach from competitors)

Pros: immediate access to already-competent, skilled workers with no training lag. Cons: requires paying far more than competitors currently pay, escalating industry-wide wage costs; disturbs equilibrium in both the organisation and industry; SCL itself was vulnerable to the same tactic and had to actively guard its own skilled workers against poaching; paying poached hires a premium creates internal pay-equity discontent; labour costs were already rising (₹963.6M → ₹1.12B) even without an aggressive poaching strategy.

Option 2 — "Train" (hire non-ceramic-background candidates, train internally)

Process: identify candidates unable to find jobs elsewhere for lack of technical skills, screen for interest in a ceramics career, run them through structured training on full salary. Pros: a continuous, controllable talent pipeline, theoretically easier to retain since trainees have fewer outside options and owe their skill formation to SCL. Cons: risk candidates remain unsuitable even after a long training program — a significant sunk cost given full-salary training; identifying and training suitable candidates is itself time-consuming and costly.

The majority of Kumar's recruitment team favoured "train," believing retention would be easier for home-grown talent; the minority worried identification and training would be too slow and costly. The case ends unresolved — SCL's salaries were already roughly in line with the industry average, yet hiring and retaining skilled talent remained fundamentally unsolved, with the clock ticking against a five-year expansion ambition.

Discussion questions implied by the case

  1. Should SCL pursue "tug," "train," or a hybrid, to build its future workforce?
  2. How should SCL quantify and compare poaching costs (wage inflation, internal equity, retaliation risk) against training costs (time, trainee salaries, post-training unsuitability risk)?
  3. How can SCL make itself more attractive to engineering graduates given the wage gap with IT/ construction and ceramics' "unconventional" image?
  4. Given the industry-wide shortage (only 7–10% of engineers employable; most preferring IT), is this a firm-level problem SCL alone can solve, or does it require an industry/policy response?
  5. How does SCL sustain a trained workforce over a five-year horizon while remaining cost-effective, given rising employee-benefit expenses?

Angles for discussion

  • Strategic: growth ambition is bottlenecked by workforce supply, not capital or demand — a live illustration of talent management as a board-level constraint.
  • Labour economics: a direct test of Cappelli's make‑versus‑buy framework — "tug" is buy, "train" is make, and the four diagnostic questions (duration of need, skill hierarchy, culture importance, forecast accuracy) apply almost exactly.
  • Employer branding: ceramics manufacturing loses a perception war against IT on pay, prestige and lifestyle that no single firm can fully solve alone — an industry-attractiveness problem, not a firm-attractiveness one.
  • Family-business governance: three generations of Somany leadership (founder, Chairman-MD, Joint MD) balance legacy identity against a professionalising HR function.
  • Financial: employee-benefit expense was already rising (₹963.6m → ₹1.12bn) before any new hiring strategy was even chosen — every option carries a direct P&L consequence.
  • Risk management: the "train" pathway's central risk — a trainee proving unsuitable after full-salary training — is precisely the scenario Kislik's "bad hire" article (this session) is built to address.
Article Session 2

What to Do When You Realize You Made a Bad Hire

Harvard Business Review · Liz Kislik · August 22, 2018

Only 19% of new hires are considered fully successful, and 46% are deemed failures by 18 months. A five-step recovery playbook: have the direct conversation, try repair via feedback or reassignment, quantify the true cost of keeping the hire, negotiate a graceful exit, and extract lessons for the future.

Full digest: the five-step playbook, the "escalation of commitment" trap, all vignettes, and the exit-management scripts

Opening statistic and framing

Only 19% of new hires are considered fully successful; by 18 months, 46% are deemed failures. This frames the core dilemma: is it worse to keep an employee who can't handle the work and is damaging the team, or to publicly admit a significant hiring mistake? Kislik's thesis: it is usually less costly to make a change, and the sooner the better — delay compounds damage.

Step 1 — Prepare for a direct, uncomfortable conversation

Rather than avoiding confrontation, level with the employee about performance concerns and invite joint problem-solving. Suggested opener: "I want to talk about the last few weeks, and where we seem to be on track and where we need to make some adjustments." Key insight: the employee may already sense the same problems and welcome addressing them collaboratively.

Step 2 — Try to repair via feedback or reassignment

Success case: a junior staffer hired for his "moxie, energy, and drive" began broadcasting dissatisfaction and making careless mistakes within weeks. His manager gave careful feedback, asked why the role felt unsatisfying, and — thanks to business growth and flexibility — shifted him to another department with a better-fitting, more challenging role, supported by intensive transition training. Failure case: a new VP hired from a different industry made repeated commitments to apply feedback but "didn't understand the business model and seemed either unable or unwilling to adapt her technical skills."

The "escalation of commitment" trap: the tendency to resist giving up on a tough situation even as evidence mounts it isn't working. Operating rule of thumb: if extensive feedback does not yield significant personal effort almost immediately, and actual improvement within three to six months, it's time to cut losses.

Step 3 — Identify the current and future expense of keeping the hire

Vignette: a senior executive from a very large public company joined a midsize family-owned client with unrealistic expectations about resources and autonomous decision-making, "costing the business dearly" — feedback didn't work, and reassignment wouldn't have solved the underlying mismatch. Cost categories to weigh against replacement/onboarding cost: reduced productivity, employee disengagement and turnover of other team members, increased interdepartmental conflict, missed deadlines and declining work quality. A subtler indicator: extra pre- and post-meeting meetings, where colleagues quietly compensate for the underperformer. A second vignette: a company restructured repeatedly trying to fit an executive who made a good first impression but lacked necessary skills, and who bottlenecked work and suppressed innovation — only after her removal did colleagues "step up to build bridges," showing the true cost of a bad hire is often invisible until after departure.

Step 4 — Make the case for an exception to the typical exit plan

If the relationship can't be salvaged, aim for the smoothest transition possible. First try to negotiate a mutually beneficial exit — an honest conversation gives the employee a sense of control while letting the manager publicly support the team and search for a replacement. Suggested script: "I appreciate your telling me how concerned you are, and the current situation is having a negative impact on the team, too, so I wonder if we can work this out in a way that benefits everyone." (Kislik explicitly notes: check with HR before doing this.) If a surprise termination is unavoidable, be direct: "As we've discussed several times, someone in your role needs to be able to accomplish these tasks and goals successfully, and you haven't been able to do that. So unfortunately, we're going to have to terminate your employment as of such-and-such date." Severance and outplacement, even outside standard policy, signal good faith to the departing employee and the remaining team — except where the employee misrepresented their skills or has ethical/behavioural problems.

Step 5 — Learn from the mistake and move quickly

It's painful for everyone when a hire goes wrong, so managers should extract lessons to avoid repeating the mistake — critical because the replacement hire must succeed. Acting deliberately but quickly benefits the departing employee too (more likely to still have other options or return to a prior role) and keeps the manager's candidate slate viable.

Practical implications for TS-ADR

Bad hires are common and costly, making selection/retention quality a genuine strategic risk. Delay increases total cost across productivity, morale and reputational/legal exposure. Managers must fight their own escalation-of-commitment bias with a hard, time-boxed test (3–6 months). Exit management should preserve dignity except where trust has been broken. This directly informs Somany Ceramics' "train" pathway: what happens if a trained recruit, after a full-salary program, still doesn't work out?

Article Session 2

Opportunities and Risks of Artificial Intelligence in Recruitment and Selection

International Journal of Organizational Analysis · Olajide Ore & Martin Sposato · 2021

Ten interviews with experienced recruiters (average 10 years' experience) at a Fortune 500 multinational manufacturer surface real opportunity — automated screening, richer candidate experience, employer branding — alongside genuine fear and distrust: job displacement, loss of the "human touch," and algorithmic bias baked in from non-representative data.

Full digest: methodology, all five themes, and every REC1–REC10 verbatim quote

Purpose and methodology

Qualitative, exploratory study via face-to-face, semi-structured, in-depth interviews with 10 professional recruiters at an unnamed Fortune 500 manufacturing MNC (90,000+ employees globally, offices in 70 countries, manufacturing in 200 countries; 50% of headcount US-based). Recruiter tenure ranged 1–11 years (mean 6); recruitment experience 4–22 years (mean 10). Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase process; recruiters coded REC1 through REC10. Central research question: "What are the opportunities and risks that will be presented if AI technology is introduced for recruitment and selection?" Emergent themes: Opportunities → data analytics, candidate experience, employer branding; Risks → fear, distrust.

Opportunity 1 — Data analytics

AI/ML tools model historical hiring decisions to screen and rank candidates, and software (Ideal, Arya, Talent Genie) scans job boards and social/professional networks to reach passive job seekers.

"Maybe, with AI automatically screening the CVs in the system and then saying that this is the best-qualified person for the job, you get a better hiring decision." (REC2)
"If AI could mine the information in the system and recognise the required keywords and then rank the best candidates for me, that would be ideal." (REC5)
"If the opportunity were there to preload questions and have a system that could pre-screen candidates based on their answers and then automatically send feedback too, it would be very useful." (REC8)

Opportunity 2 — Candidate experience

Large application volumes limit recruiters' ability to engage candidates individually; AI is seen as a way to preserve positive candidate perception at scale.

"We would really be able to focus on the candidate experience by making use of data analytics… to provide candidates with a first-class experience when engaging with the organization." (REC6)
"AI will allow us to take advantage of the free time that we will have so that we can improve the candidate experience." (REC10)

Opportunity 3 — Employer branding

"I feel that we will be more engaged with employer branding initiatives because that is really where the competition lies. The war for talent will be to focus on attracting candidates in terms of what our company can offer to them and the kind of culture that we have." (REC3)
"I think AI would really allow us to have an opportunity to positively market our core company values to attract candidates." (REC7)

Risk 1 — Fear

Fear concerns AI reliability/accuracy, data-privacy anxieties, discrimination potential, and loss of the "human touch" or displacement of human recruiters.

"I am worried because we don't know what the limit of AI is, so there will be challenges around the privacy of people's personal data… people have the right to have their private lives." (REC2)
"With the introduction of AI, the loss of human interaction in the process is something that the MNC and the wider society should really be concerned about." (REC4)
"We could face ethical issues because of discrimination, mainly because I also think AI has its own potential for bias, and this is a big issue for me." (REC7)

Risk 2 — Distrust

Centres on accuracy/reliability and the possibility that AI perpetuates or amplifies human bias.

"There can be biases in how the algorithms are programmed, so if the person who is feeding information to the AI is discriminating against certain people or groups of people, then AI would do the same." (REC6)
"There's no standard way to write a CV, so potentially good candidates may not be screened correctly if they don't have the right keywords on their CV. So, I would not trust AI to be able to find those good candidates who do not know how to write a good CV." (REC10)

Literature threads underpinning the risk theme

Cappelli (2001, p.144) warns screening technology could "reject a disproportionate number of underrepresented groups, including women, minority ethnics, disabled people and workers older than forty years of age." Hurlburt (2017) warns of coders embedding cultural/personal bias into algorithms. Žliobaitė and Custers (2016) note algorithms can discriminate "even if the computing process is 'fair and well-intentioned'" due to biased or non-representative training data. Counter-view — Housman (2016): algorithmic selection could actually reduce bias, given recruiters' natural tendency to hire people similar to themselves.

Discussion and conclusions

AI promises to mitigate bias but could equally exacerbate it via embedded human bias in training data. AI's efficiency gains free recruiters for strategic work, better candidate experience and stronger employer branding — but the authors warn against a "blind faith" approach that risks irreparable brand damage and discrimination-related fines. All ten recruiters believed human interaction will always be needed; the paper's conclusion is that recruiters should become "AI advocates," not resisters, repositioning themselves as leaders of AI-enabled transformation.

Article Session 2

The Make‑Versus‑Buy Decision: Deciding Whether to Hire Internally or From the Outside

Excerpted from Talent on Demand, Harvard Business Press · Peter Cappelli · 2008

Reframes talent management as an operations-research "newsvendor problem": the real question isn't make vs. buy, but which forecasting error — overshooting or undershooting talent supply — costs more, since idle "benched" talent is usually a bigger flight risk than a temporary shortfall. Offers four diagnostic questions and a real-options framework for deciding the right blend.

Full digest: the IT boom-bust history, the wage-value model, mismatch costs, the four diagnostics, and the worked real-options example

Talent management as supply-chain management

Cappelli's central analogy: failing to systematically manage talent is like failing to manage a supply chain — but the stakes are higher, since a quitting/retiring employee can lose clients or stall work. The "bumping problem": a typical poor response to a vacancy — "does anyone here can take over?" — creates a chain reaction of internal moves (citing Diane Rothbard Margolis's 1970s finding that a single vacancy triggered five additional job changes at one company), each risking placing someone in a role they're unprepared for. Outside hiring avoids bumping but leaves roles vacant during search, is expensive, and blocks internal promotion paths.

The IT industry boom-bust cautionary tale

Traditional, planning-heavy firms (IBM, Hewlett-Packard, Sperry, Univac, and Route 128 firms like Wang, Data General, DEC) relied on internal development; 1990s Silicon Valley firms relied on outside hiring, luring talent with stock options — and beat Route 128 firms partly because they could hire talent away without upfront development investment (citing Saxenian's Regional Advantage). Real wages for IT grads peaked in 1986, then declined amid the 1991 recession; IT-degree enrolment fell through the mid-1990s just as the market then exploded into a red-hot mid-90s talent shortage. Software engineer turnover averaged ~40% per year. IT-major enrolment rebounded sharply (+40% in 1996–97, +39% in 1997–98) — but the market crashed in 2001 just as this cohort graduated, creating a talent surplus. Only 52% of civil engineering graduates stayed in that field 20 years post-graduation versus just 19% of computer science graduates; a George Mason study found IT workers twice as likely to change careers as other occupations. Age-bias evidence: an Information Week survey found only 2% of IT managers would hire someone with 10+ years' experience; a Network World survey found only 13% of 20–30-year-olds would hire anyone over 40.

The swing back to internal development

By the late 1990s many employers concluded pure outside hiring wasn't working. The Co-Operative Group had been filling up to 80% of executive vacancies via outside search; CEO Martin Beaumont stated: "Our goal is to get to the point where 70 percent of those appointments are internal" — a near-total reversal.

The newsvendor problem and mismatch costs

Cappelli imports the classic operations-research "newsvendor problem": a forecast will never be perfect, and firms face asymmetric costs — too little talent means unfilled roles and lost business; too much means an expensive, underused "human capital supply closet." Unlike physical inventory, excess talent can walk away, taking your training investment with it — an employee "on the bench" is primed to job-hunt, especially once a competitor offers immediate use of her skills.

Supporting evidence: the Towers Perrin 2006 global survey (86,000 employees) found 15% actively trying to leave and 43% open to other opportunities; career development/advancement was the leading driver of both retention and engagement. Watson-Wyatt studies found one of the best predictors of turnover was whether an employee had recently received training — trained employees were more likely to leave, poached by competitors who benefit without paying for the training. Wharton students reported they would wait only an average of 10 months for a development opportunity before concluding advancement was blocked and moving on. Sidebar: GE's famous talent-development machine produced so many capable leaders (7 former GE HR executives left to head HR elsewhere, 1998–2000) that Cappelli reframes this celebrated fact as potentially representing excess talent inventory — GE effectively subsidises other firms' leadership needs.

The wage-value model and the 1970s/1980s crisis

The classic internal-labour-market model: employee value contribution rises over a career (thanks to training investment) while employment cost is set by outside market wages and engineered upward mainly via promotions (the Hay Company's "Hay System" of job evaluation). Three career phases: early years (employer invests, cost exceeds value), middle years (value rises faster than pay, employer recoups investment), late career (seniority pay plus performance plateau means employer pays more than value received). This explains the 1970s crisis: too many managers were in the pipeline for too few roles once growth slowed. The 1980s model broke down as employers began hiring experienced outside workers who could be paid more (no training investment to recoup), forcing training employers into a no-win choice: raise wages or lose staff. Executive compensation escalation compounded this: average US CEO pay rose from $1.3 million (1993) to $2.5 million (2003) — a 7.1% annual increase, more than double general wage growth — meaning executives increasingly capture the returns on their own development rather than the firm doing so.

The make-or-buy choice: not binary, but a portfolio decision

Employer-provided training rates fall when unemployment rises (surplus outside labour makes hiring cheaper than developing). Firms with proprietary technology or a strong impenetrable culture lean "make"; firms needing wholly new skills or deliberate culture change must "buy" — e.g., the Nuclear Regulatory Commission directing firms with safety problems to hire new managers from the Navy specifically to change culture. Cost structure: internal development front-loads costs but the advantage grows over tenure; external hires cost little upfront but carry higher ongoing market-rate pay — buying's advantage is greatest for short-term/uncertain needs and dissipates over time.

The four diagnostic questions

  1. How long will you need the talent? Longer need → easier to recoup development investment → lean "make."
  2. Is there a learnable skill hierarchy? More hierarchy → easier to develop internally → lean "make."
  3. How important is preserving vs. changing culture? Preserve culture → "make"; want deliberate change → "buy" (though the direction of change from multiple simultaneous outside hires can be hard to control).
  4. How accurate is your demand forecast? Less certainty → higher risk of internal development → lean "buy."

Implication: developing talent cost-effectively requires scale and a reasonably predictable product market — explaining why talent-development-famous firms tend to be large, functionally organised, in industries with long product cycles (oil exploration, pharmaceuticals). IBM found itself with an oversupply in its high-potential program and cut it in half to rebalance toward outside hiring. "Buy" also extends to leasing employees via staffing agencies and outsourcing — the contractor absorbs supply-side risk, but the client still bears demand-forecast risk and pays a premium: "outsourcing offers no free lunch for employers."

Mismatch costs — asymmetric, not symmetric

The old "Organization Man" era worried only about falling short; the modern challenge requires balancing both directions. Undershoot costs have fallen (gaps filled via lateral/outside hiring); overshoot costs have risen (benched employees are retention risks, and "excess" talent looks like "fat" to restructuring-minded managers — the line between "fat" and "a deep bench" is, in Cappelli's words, "in the eye of the beholder"). Sidebar: Unilever India — a model internal-promotion employer since the 1950s, running a "listers" fast-track program and a three-candidate promotion tournament (winner gets the job, runner-up an alternative assignment, third can leave). Around 2000, restructuring flattened the org pyramid and slowed advancement, but high relative pay made executives reluctant to quit — so by 2004 the division had 1,400 managers, up from 1,100 in 2000, even as demand had declined; Unilever ultimately transferred the surplus managers to its international operations.

Estimating talent demand — practical method

Stop obsessing over a single point estimate ("we'll need exactly 100 programmers"); instead assess the cost consequences of both overshoot and undershoot. Ask: what odds would you take that actual demand will be within 10% either way of forecast? What would a 10% shortfall cost to cover via outside hiring? What would a 10% overshoot cost in idle time and retention? Worked example: if overshoot risk exceeds undershoot risk for 100 needed programmers, develop fewer internally (e.g., 90) and hire the shortfall (~10) externally; if the estimate is more of a guess, develop only ~60 and hire the rest. Zensar Technologies (Vivek Gupta, Senior VP) initially undershot internal development fearing retention problems, then switched to deliberately overshooting once the labour market tightened and outside hiring became harder — because undershooting had become the costlier error.

Real options framework

Borrowing finance's real-options logic — the value of preserving the right (not obligation) to act later — applied to talent: is it worth maintaining idle capability now because of its option value later? Worked example: retaining 2 key recruiters for a year with no current workload costs $100,000; probability business picks up enough to need them: 10%; if it does, having them in place versus rebuilding from scratch saves an estimated $4.2 million against a $1 million full build-out cost — net benefit if exercised = $3.2 million; expected value of the option = 10% × $3.2M = $320,000, which exceeds the $100,000 retention cost, so retaining the recruiters is worthwhile even with only a 1-in-10 chance of needing them.

Concluding synthesis

Neither pure internal development nor pure outside hiring works as a standalone strategy. The right approach is an explicit, quantified portfolio — blending internal development, outside hiring, leasing/temp staffing and outsourcing — guided by the four diagnostic questions, explicit attention to asymmetric mismatch costs (oversupply now typically costlier than undersupply), and a real-options lens for preserving capability against uncertain future need. The chapter reframes talent management as a risk-management/operations-research problem, not a purely developmental or cultural one.

Report Session 3

The Future of Jobs Report 2025

World Economic Forum, Insight Report · Centre for the New Economy and Society · January 2025

A survey of 1,000+ employers (14M+ workers, 55 economies) projects 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced by 2030 (net +78 million), with skills gaps cited by 63% of employers as the top barrier to transformation — ranking #1 in 52 of 55 economies surveyed.

Full digest: methodology, all five macrotrends, jobs/skills tables, workforce-strategy barriers, DEI and wage data, and the India-specific findings

Overview and methodology

The fifth edition of WEF's biannual report, built on the Future of Jobs Survey (late 2024) covering 1,000+ employers representing 14.1 million workers across 22 industry clusters and 55 economies, enriched with data from ADP, Coursera, Indeed and LinkedIn. Projects labour-market change over 2025–2030. Macro backdrop: global growth projected at 3.2% for 2025; global unemployment at 4.9% (lowest since 1991); youth unemployment 13% globally; the ILO "jobs gap" measure implies 402 million additional jobs needed in 2024.

The five macrotrends (share of employers expecting each to transform their business by 2030)

  1. Broadening digital access — 60% (top overall trend, a critical enabler for all other technology-driven transformation)
  2. Rising cost of living / inflation — 50%
  3. Climate mitigation investment — 47%
  4. Increased focus on labour and social issues — 46%
  5. Slower economic growth — 42%
  6. Climate adaptation investment — 41%
  7. Ageing and declining working-age populations — 40%
  8. Increased geopolitical division and conflicts — 34%
  9. Growing working-age populations — 24%
  10. Increased restrictions to global trade and investment — 23%

These map to five overarching categories: technological change, economic uncertainty, green transition, demographic shifts, and geoeconomic fragmentation.

Technological change

Top technologies cited: AI and information processing (86%), robots and autonomous systems (58%), energy generation/storage/distribution (41%). AI investment has risen nearly eightfold since ChatGPT's November 2022 release. Global robot density reached 162 units per 10,000 employees in 2023 — double the level seven years earlier — with 80% of installations concentrated in China, Japan, the US, South Korea and Germany. India and the US lead GenAI training enrolments (Coursera data), though in India corporate sponsorship — not individual initiative — drives the surge.

Economic uncertainty

2025 growth projected at 3.2%, with downward revisions concentrated in low-income economies. Services inflation remains roughly twice pre-pandemic levels, acute in low-income countries facing food-price shocks.

Geoeconomic fragmentation

WTO: trade restrictions doubled between 2020 and 2024; import restrictions reached ~10% of global imports in 2024. IMF estimates potential global output losses of 0.2%–7% of GDP from trade fragmentation. Geoeconomically affected employers are more likely to re-shore than off-shore (e.g., trade-restriction-affected firms: 8.7% off-shore vs. 14.5% re-shore).

Green transition

Green-skill acquisition rose 12% globally (2022–2023) but green-skill job postings rose ~22% in the same period — demand outpacing supply. Green-skilled workers are hired at a consistently higher rate than the overall labour market.

Demographic shifts

Two simultaneous global shifts: ageing/declining working-age populations in high-income economies, and expanding populations in lower-income economies (India, Sub-Saharan Africa). The global working-age population split, currently 49% lower-income/51% higher-income, is projected to shift to 59% lower-income by 2050. World Bank: over the next decade, 1.2 billion young people in emerging economies will reach working age, but these economies are projected to create only 420 million additional jobs — risking ~800 million young people in economic uncertainty.

Jobs outlook

Structural labour-market churn equals 22% of today's 1.2 billion formal jobs over 2025–2030: 170 million new jobs created (14%), 92 million displaced (8%), net growth of 78 million (7%). Fastest-growing roles (percentage terms): Big Data Specialists, FinTech Engineers, AI and Machine Learning Specialists, Software and Applications Developers, Security Management Specialists. Fastest-declining roles: Data Entry Clerks, Bank Tellers, Postal Service Clerks, Cashiers, Administrative Assistants and Executive Secretaries — notably, Graphic Designers and Legal Secretaries appear in decline for the first time in the report's history, read as evidence of GenAI's growing capacity for knowledge work. Largest absolute job growth: Farmworkers and Agricultural Labourers (+35 million by 2030), followed by Delivery Drivers, Construction Workers, Salespersons; largest absolute declines concentrate in clerical/secretarial roles.

By macrotrend: broadening digital access creates the most and destroys the most jobs of any single trend (+19M created/–9M displaced); robotics is the largest net job displacer (net –5M); slower economic growth is the only macrotrend that is a net job destroyer overall.

Automation vs. augmentation: today, tasks split 47% human-only, 22% technology-only, 30% combination. By 2030, employers expect a near-even three-way split (~33–34% each). Of the ~15-point reduction in human-only task share, ~82% is attributable to automation and ~19% to augmentation/collaboration.

Skills outlook

Employers expect 39% of workers' core skills to change or become outdated by 2030 — down from 44% in 2023 and a pandemic-era peak of 57% in 2020. Top core skills for 2025: analytical thinking (69%), resilience/flexibility/agility (67%), leadership and social influence (61%), creative thinking (57%), motivation and self-awareness (52%). Fastest-growing skills 2025–2030 (net increase): AI and big data (+87), networks and cybersecurity (+70), technological literacy (+68), creative thinking (+66), resilience/flexibility/agility (+66). The largest net decline: manual dexterity, endurance and precision (–24) — the first time physical/manual skills have shown a net negative trajectory. Of 2,800+ granular skills assessed for GenAI substitution capacity (Indeed/ GPT-4o analysis), zero scored "very high capacity" for substitution and 69% scored "low" or "very low" — skills with no substitution potential include empathy/active listening, sensory-processing, and manual dexterity. Training data: 50% of the global workforce has completed training as part of learning/ development initiatives (up from 41% in 2023); if the workforce were 100 people, 41 would not need significant training by 2030, 29 could be upskilled in-role, 19 upskilled and redeployed, and 11 would be unlikely to receive needed training (at risk).

Workforce strategies and barriers

Top barriers to transformation: skills gaps in the labour market (63%, ranked #1 in 52 of 55 economies), organisational culture and resistance to change (46%), outdated/inflexible regulation (39%), inability to attract talent to the industry (37%). Talent-outlook net expectations: talent availability when hiring: net –13% (only 29% expect improvement, down sharply from 39% in 2023); talent development: net +66%; talent retention: net +25% (down from 53% in 2023). Planned workforce strategies: upskill workforce (85%, top priority across all geographies), accelerate automation (73%), hire staff with new skills (70%), augment workforce with new technologies (63%). Practices to improve talent availability: supporting employee health and well-being jumped from 9th place (2023) to 1st (64%, 2025); tapping into diverse talent pools rose from ~10% to 47% — a roughly fourfold increase.

DEI and wages

83% of employers now have DEI measures in place (up from 67% in 2023), near-universal among firms with 50,000+ employees (95%). Priority DEI groups: women (76%), workers with disabilities (56%), Gen Z under 25 (52%). On wages: 52% of employers expect wages to grow as a share of revenue by 2030; smaller firms (<1,000 employees) are the most bullish (57%). Skills-assessment approaches: work-experience evaluation still dominant (81%); skills/pre-employment tests (48%); university degree requirement (43%) — signalling gradual de-emphasis of credentials, since 14 of the 15 fastest-growing jobs still nominally require a degree while only 7 of the 15 largest-growing jobs require an advanced one.

India-specific findings

India's primary shaping trends: increased digital access, geopolitical tensions, and climate-mitigation efforts. Indian employers are investing heavily in AI, robotics and energy technologies and are expected to outpace global adoption in specific technologies — 35% expect semiconductors/computing to transform operations and 21% expect quantum/encryption to do so, both above global shares. India's fastest-growing roles (Big Data Specialists, AI/ML Specialists, Security Management Specialists) align closely with these trends. To address talent needs, Indian employers place unusually high emphasis on tapping diverse talent pools (67% vs. 47% globally) and skills-based hiring / removing degree requirements (30% vs. 19% globally) — both substantially above global averages, positioning India as a leading adopter of skills-first hiring.

Conclusion / exam takeaways

The report frames 2025–2030 as a period of net job growth (+7%) but severe skills churn (39%) and negative talent-availability sentiment (–13% net) — jobs are being created, but employers doubt their ability to find the right people. Skills gaps (63%) dominate as the strategic barrier, ahead of capital, regulation, or culture, reframing talent strategy (upskilling, skills-based hiring, diverse pools) as central to business transformation. GenAI shows near-zero current capacity to replace deeply human/ physical skills, reframing resilience, leadership, creative thinking and empathy as durable differentiators.

Article Session 3

Predictive HR Analytics and Talent Management: A Conceptual Framework

Journal of Management Analytics · R. Navodya Gurusinghe, Bhadra J. H. Arachchige & Dushar Dayarathna · 2021

Fuses the Technology-Organisation-Environment framework with Resource-Based Theory to explain what it actually takes to move from basic HR reporting to genuine predictive capability — and why a data-driven culture, not just technology spend, is the real bottleneck.

Full digest: the TOE+RBT model, all seven adoption antecedents, Bersin's maturity model, regulatory comparisons, and the talent-outcome propositions

Purpose and theoretical foundations

A conceptual (non-empirical) paper — "possibly the first" to propose a theoretical model linking the antecedents of HR-analytics adoption, the process of building Predictive HR Analytics (PHRA) capability, and talent-management outcomes, moderated by data-driven organisational culture. It fuses the Technology-Organisation-Environment (TOE) framework (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990) — three contexts (technological, organisational, environmental) shaping adoption — with Resource-Based Theory (Barney, 1991) — sustainable advantage from resources that are Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, and Organised (VRIO) — because TOE explains adoption but not how analytics becomes a strategic capability, while RBT explains capability-building but ignores external/ regulatory factors.

The motivating statistics

Citing Deloitte's 2017 Global Human Capital Trends survey: 71% of companies see HR analytics as a pressing concern, yet only 8% report having usable data, only 9% believe they understand which talent factors drive performance, and just 15% have implemented HR/talent scorecards for line managers — illustrating an "analytics paradox" where investment and interest far outpace actual capability.

The seven adoption antecedents, across three TOE contexts

Technological context — (1) Technology competence: infrastructure plus IT-skilled staff. Aral et al. (2012) found no performance effect from HR analytics without genuine Human Capital Management (HCM) software. Kaur & Fink (2017), from 22 interviews across 16 organisations, found the most-used tools were R (94%), Tableau (83%), Python (50%), SPSS (44%), Excel (44%) — standard HRIS cannot support advanced statistics/visualisation. Named AI tools: IBM's Watson Talent, Intel's Saffron, HireVue's video-analytics platform.

Organisational context — (2) Basic resources (sufficient financial investment and time, since HR analytics rarely yields immediate ROI); (3) Analytical competencies of HR personnel (ability to build causal logic, use appropriate statistical tests, and embed insights into decisions — most HR staff are not trained data scientists but need enough methodology/statistics literacy to persuade sceptical executives); (4) Top management commitment (C-suite support supplies resources and a change-friendly environment; firms with strong PHRA maturity are more likely to have a CHRO on the board or a people-analytics Centre of Excellence reporting to the CEO); (5) HR-business strategic alignment (analytics projects must connect to the firm's core value chain and be presented in business language, not just HR metrics).

Environmental context — (6) Regulatory support: a key cross-country comparison — in the US, employee data collected at work is legally the company's property; in the EU, organisations must justify why they need to collect employees' personal data; in Canada, it is illegal to monitor employees' private emails. (7) Competitive pressure (Porter & Millar, 1985): if peer firms use HR analytics for talent management, a firm feels urgency to adopt or risk losing competitive edge in the talent-shortage battle.

Josh Bersin's (2016) four-stage maturity model

  1. Operational reporting — 56% of firms (2016). Basic metrics (headcount, turnover, labour cost); data siloed, reporting reactive.
  2. Advanced reporting — 30% of firms. Both operational and advanced metrics; reporting becomes proactive, embedded in daily operations, but still largely descriptive.
  3. Advanced analytics — 10% of firms. Statistical models supporting decisions; the first stage adding genuine strategic value.
  4. Predictive analytics — the rarest stage. Enables scenario planning and proactive risk mitigation — identifying future talent shortages, high-potential talent, and critical positions; requires tools like R and dedicated data scientists.

Data-driven organisational culture — the moderating variable

Defined as the extent to which organisational members at all levels make decisions based on data insight. LaValle et al. (2011) and Ross et al. (2013) argue it is organisational culture — not data quality or technology — that is usually why unproductive big-data projects fail; many firms still rely on the "highest-paid person's opinion" (HiPPO) rather than data. Proposition: a firm can adopt analytics tools without a data-driven culture, but cannot progress to true predictive capability without one.

PHRA capability → talent management outcomes

Talent management is decomposed into three lifecycle elements — acquisition (attracting/hiring high-potential workforce), development (training/succession planning), and retention (motivating/engaging talent) — each proposed to be positively related to PHRA capability. PricewaterhouseCoopers (2020): 74% of global CEOs cite "a lack of availability of the right skills" as a concern, reinforcing the urgency for predictive tools. The paper cites Walford-Wright & Scott-Jackson's "Talent Rising" model (this session's third reading) as direct empirical corroboration that a technology-driven talent-acquisition strategy measurably improves key acquisition metrics.

Implications and limitations

Fills a gap Marler & Boudreau (2017) identified — no theory-based prediction of relationships and no data collected to evaluate them in HR analytics literature. Practical implications: guides which resources/skills are needed, how to build the case for top-management buy-in, and how analytics vendors should target adoption antecedents. The model remains untested empirically and does not examine the ultimate link from data-driven talent decisions to overall organisational performance.

Article Session 3

Talent Rising: People Analytics and Technology Driving Talent Acquisition Strategy

Strategic HR Review · Gavin Walford‑Wright & William Scott‑Jackson · 2018

A practitioner case study of "Talent Rising," an end-to-end recruitment technology stack built to cut cost- and time-per-hire while raising quality of hire — automating routine screening while keeping judgement-heavy steps (final interviews, offers) human-led.

Full digest: the diagnosed starting condition, the full process architecture, all four selection tools, and the closing "technology lust" caution

Purpose and framing

Proposes the "Talent Rising" model: a modular, "plug and play" technology stack designed for three objectives that map onto Porter's (1985) generic strategies — reduce time to hire (innovation), reduce cost per hire (cost leadership), and increase quality of hire (quality/differentiation). Framing quote from Gerd Leonhard (2016): "humanity will change more in the next 20 years than in the previous 300." The paper updates Michaels, Handfield-Jones & Axelrod's "War for Talent" thesis for the digital era, arguing candidate centricity is the new source of competitive advantage.

The worker-to-retiree ratio statistic

The 2015 McKinsey Global Growth Model study (2005–2015) found three workers for every retiree; the authors project this ratio will fall to 1:1 within a decade — shrinking the candidate pool dramatically by 2025, compounded by demand for specialised talent in technology and life sciences. Implication: candidates, not employers, hold the power in the hiring relationship, making candidate experience a critical differentiator.

Diagnosed starting-condition problems in talent-acquisition teams

Ten recurring deficiencies identified across organisations studied: no clear vision; no Applicant Tracking System; low engagement; no people analytics; no referral programme; high cost per hire (over-reliance on agencies); long time to hire (manual handoffs); lower quality of hire (poor/no assessments); poor trust with hiring managers; and no KPIs or Individual Development Plans for the talent-acquisition team itself.

People analytics and talent technology

Defined: "People analytics uses data to inform management decisions, be it in talent acquisition, retention, placement, promotion, compensation, or workforce and succession planning." Fewer than half of HR leaders believe they successfully link people data to key business/financial data. Google, Best Buy, P&G and Sysco were already using sophisticated data analysis to remove guesswork from employee management as early as 2010 (Davenport, Harris & Shapiro, HBR). Talent technology is defined as "the point where systems and processes of human resources management and technology intersect." A quarter of organisations reported technology was already replacing some jobs internally; Gartner's 2014 prediction that Big Data/analytics would generate $3.7 trillion in products/services and 4.4 million new jobs by 2015 is cited as validated.

The Talent Rising process architecture

Sourcing/candidate attraction — reaching passive candidates via LinkedIn and social platforms, with candidates encouraged to build a "3D online portrait" (photos, recommendations, video, interactive résumé) → candidate experience/concierge services (GPS guidance, interviewer background info, geo-location beacon notifications, campus tours) → automated front-end selection tools → a Talent Acquisition Review (governance checkpoint synthesising sourcing and assessment data) → onboarding.

The four selection tools and their purposes

  1. Organisational culture-fit tool — for sifting: does the candidate fit the culture?
  2. Cognitive ability assessments — for sifting: can they learn quickly and grow into the role? The paper argues cognitive ability may be "the single most valuable assessment in the talent acquisition process" for many roles.
  3. Video interviewing tools — for screening.
  4. Personality tools — for final-stage selecting.

The paper cites Bock's (2015) Work Rules! (drawing on his experience as Google's SVP of People Operations): hiring "doesn't matter who you are, where you're from or what school you went to" — organisations should actively seek non-traditional candidates rather than screening on human bias.

Predictive use of people analytics and Google's example

Talent technology's greatest HR value is predictive: analysing both high- and low-performer attributes builds a template for future hires — modelling underperformance is as valuable as modelling excellence. The resulting "3D" candidate view — seen and heard, backed by factual assessment — is described as democratic, impartial, and supportive of meritocracy. Google's People Analytics function is the exemplar, measuring social skills, flexibility, emotional intelligence, initiative, attitude and perseverance.

Named quotes

Dan Shapero, LinkedIn's VP of Talent Solutions and Insights: "recruiting has always been an art, but it's becoming a science."
Dr Brian Welle, Google's Director of People Analytics: "if we have an employee base that reflects our [diverse] user base, we are going to better understand the needs of people all over the world."

Risks/caveats and the closing "technology lust" caution

Candidates with low computer skills could be disadvantaged by tech-heavy selection — the authors reframe this as an acceptable filter for tech-based organisations, where "tech savvy" is itself a job requirement. The talent-acquisition team's own skills must be developed to properly interpret assessment outputs — technology adoption alone doesn't guarantee good hiring outcomes. The paper's closing caution:

"There is a counterbalance for this lust for technology and people analytics… technology initiatives should be considered in view of the human factor that is the real driver of long-term success. People, not technology, are responsible for ensuring a steady progress toward the goal of attaining organisational excellence… The tipping point is where futurism meets humanity, and in attracting talent to an organisation, technology can make or break the talent connection."

Practical payoff claimed: implementation produced "a significant improvement in all the key metrics related to the talent acquisition process" and changed the recruiter/TA-team role from transactional gatekeeper to internal strategic partner.
