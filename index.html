Article Session 1

Talent Management and the Future of Work

Harvard Business School Note 9‑820‑084 · William Kerr & Gorick Ng · February 14, 2020

A framework note that traces three colliding "tectonic plates" — technology advancement, demographic transition, and global integration — to three workforce questions every manager must answer (access, competencies, productivity), then hands managers a menu of 15 concrete levers across talent management, workplace design, and external collaborations.

Full breakdown: the three forces of change, the three workforce challenges each force creates, and the complete 15-lever managerial framework, with every named example and quote from the note.

Framing: three tectonic plates

Kerr and Ng open with the observation that Amazon and AT&T have each announced plans to invest billions of dollars retraining nearly half of their respective workforces for jobs of the future — the note's opening hook for why talent strategy can no longer be reactive. The authors liken technology advancement, demographic transition, and global integration to tectonic plates rubbing against each other, interacting in unprecedented ways to affect firms' access to the right workers with the right skills, and to redefine what worker productivity even means. The note's stated purpose is to summarize the key drivers of change and offer a framework managers can apply to their own businesses.

Force 1: Technology Advancement

The scale of change is illustrated with a single comparison: Apple's 2018 iPhone had 100,000 times the processing power of the room-sized computers NASA used for the Apollo program in the 1960s, while being 3,300 times cheaper and small enough to fit in one's hand. This "growing, compounding, cheapening, ubiquity, and consumerization of computing power" — alongside Wi-Fi, cloud computing, connected devices, machine learning, and robotics — is reshaping how goods are produced, services delivered, and work performed. The note lists five specific technology-driven shifts, each paired with the managerial imperative it creates:

  1. The digitization of nearly every industry, function, and business process — fueling the need to transform business models, organizational structures, and legacy practices.
  2. The decline in barriers to entry for technology-first players with different cost structures — fueling the need to rethink one's value chain, value proposition, and business model.
  3. The pressure for human labor to be automated or augmented by technology — fueling the need to reevaluate how work gets done and how proper technique is taught.
  4. The unlocking of an unprecedented amount of latent data and insights — fueling the need to adopt more analytical methods and cultivate more digitally savvy workers.
  5. The declining half-life of worker skills — fueling the need to continually ensure that workers' skills are sharp, relevant, and up to date.

The competitive landscape, the authors argue, is tilting toward data- and technology-savvy firms with robust technological infrastructure, skilled and adaptable workers, and cultures of experimentation. They cite Tim Buckley (HBS 1996), CEO of The Vanguard Group, discussing how technology-enabled financial advisory services are reshaping Vanguard's core business:

Tim Buckley, CEO, The Vanguard Group: "Technology will change the advice industry, and that's uncomfortable. You can fight it, you can deny it, or you can embrace it as an opportunity to scale your business better and lower your cost structure."

Force 2: Demographic Transition

Unlike technology, the authors stress that demographic change is not uncertain — it is already locked in. Female labor-force entry, historically a natural way to expand the workforce, has "mostly run its course," with participation rates having peaked in the late 20th century. Birth rates, which peaked in the 1950s, have since fallen to historic lows, pushing retirements to outpace new worker entry. Millennials already comprise 35% of the workforce — the largest share of any single generation — and are notably well-educated with new preferences about work. Educated workers have also decisively migrated to urban centers in search of opportunity, and the workforce as a whole has never been more diverse in composition. Five resulting shifts and their managerial implications:

  1. Slower growth (or outright decline) in national workforces — fueling the need to identify and compete for scarcer and often hidden workers.
  2. The rising age and impending retirement of many workers — fueling the need to succession plan, fill vacancies, and support workers' caregiving burdens.
  3. Growing workplace diversity in age, ethnicity, and background — raising the importance of appreciating different worker needs and team capabilities.
  4. Changing attitudes and expectations of Millennials and Gen Z toward work — raising the need to rethink the firm-worker relationship and one's value proposition as an employer.
  5. Growing concentration of economic power and skilled workers within large cities — fueling the need to bridge concentrated talent pools with broader workforces.

The note quotes Greg Case (HBS 1989), CEO of Aon Corporation, on why people strategy and business strategy must converge in "best-in-class" firms:

Greg Case, CEO, Aon Corporation: "People allocation is as powerful as financial allocation."

Force 3: Global Integration

Since World War II, technological advancement and globalization have reinforced one another, with rising information and communications technology both enabling and resulting from the explosion of multinational firms. As emerging markets industrialize and climb the income ladder, business has become "unprecedentedly global — and globally competitive." Five shifts:

  1. The opportunity to access an increasingly global base of customers, clients, and partners — fueling the need to build overseas capabilities and be sensitive to local differences.
  2. The ability to access an increasingly international talent pool, at home and abroad — fueling the need to navigate immigration laws and manage a globally distributed workforce.
  3. The potential for work to be done anytime, anywhere, by a broader range of workers — fueling the need to redefine productivity and navigate remote and complex team structures.
  4. Continued economic pressure to place routine support work in lower-cost settings — fueling the need to manage regulatory, reputational, and operational risks.
  5. The increasing emergence of formidable overseas competitors with global ambition — fueling the need to safeguard intellectual property and track foreign rivals and trends.

The authors are careful to note globalization's uneven distribution of gains: "alarming levels of economic inequality" have fueled populism, nationalism, and protectionism worldwide, with Brexit cited as a case where these forces threatened to stall or reverse global integration. Against this, they quote Jamie Dimon (HBS 1982), CEO of JPMorgan Chase, striking an optimistic but qualified note:

Jamie Dimon, CEO, JPMorgan Chase: "Globalization — I don't think it has to be reversed… there were flaws and maybe some unfair trading practices and… people were hurt by it. So you go to some towns in America and people lost their jobs. And, if they didn't lose them, they went from earning $40 an hour to $10 an hour. We need to recognize that and do something about it… think of retraining, relocation, business redevelopment — all these things which you know can work if properly done."

Three Workplace Challenges

The note translates the three forces into three questions every manager increasingly must confront: (1) "Do we have access to workers?" (2) "Do these workers have the right competencies and skills?" and (3) "Is this talent productive?"

Challenge 1 — Worker Access

Firms access workers through four channels, each with its own tradeoffs:

  • Recruit on campus — partnering with colleges/universities for internships, co-ops, scholarships, apprenticeships, and full-time offers. Benefits include cultural assimilation and "promote from within," but firms struggle with high hiring costs from competing against better-resourced/branded rivals, the relative inexperience of hires requiring foundational-skills investment, churn risk among trained interns and new hires, and the need to offer cultures aligned with younger workers' expectations.
  • Hire active and passive job seekers — posting jobs and using recruiters/referrals to attract experienced "passive candidates." Challenges: reliance on poor, bias-ridden proxies to evaluate fit; dependence on brand name recognition; the need to offer wages passive candidates "cannot refuse"; and the risk that new hires resemble the existing workforce, adding little diversity.
  • Encourage entry of "hidden workers" outside the labor force — the previously incarcerated, those recovering from substance abuse, the physically impaired, and those re-entering after parental leave or retirement. Obstacles: reimagining hiring funnels, training for employment gaps, restructuring working norms around personal lives, and addressing stigma toward non-traditional applicants.
  • Reallocate internal talent to better-fitting roles — enabled by algorithms that match gig workers to projects and by employees' rising appetite to switch roles. Requires identifying/mapping/training for role-specific skills, overcoming institutional inertia and funding constraints, and managing friction from less-traditional career paths.
Challenge 2 — Competencies and Skills

Tight labor markets force firms to reevaluate candidate screening. The note flags "degree inflation" — job postings requiring more education than incumbents actually possess — as a phenomenon that needlessly inflates labor costs and shrinks the qualified applicant pool, while also noting that the "lack of career readiness among college graduates" undermines the degree as a reliable signal of future productivity. Two specific gaps are examined:

  • The soft skills gap — as automation absorbs rule-based tasks and complements complex problem-solving and communication work, demand for soft skills like teamwork rises. Named examples: Walmart uses virtual-reality headsets to train employees on customer service; McDonald's is elevating the hiring of retirees, who tend to be more reliable and professional than younger workers. The struggle involves granularly identifying and teaching the attitudes/behaviors that separate effective from mediocre workers, overcoming the belief that soft skills are hard to teach or quantify for ROI purposes, and confronting the reality that soft skills function as "cultural capital" — tastes and mannerisms of a dominant class not shared by all in diverse workplaces.
  • The digital skills gap — illustrated by the fact that a modern John Deere tractor now has 30–40 on-board computers. This demands not just technical literacy but "digital soft skills" like customer-centricity, data-driven decision making, and comfort with ambiguity — needed even by outsourced software developers "an ocean away" who must "manage up" and navigate institutional interdependencies. Challenges: managing large numbers of non-digital natives less open to change and less fluent in digital language; allocating time/funding to upskill workers while accepting not everyone reaches proficiency; and navigating transformations that are costly, disruptive, and likely to provoke resistance and job cuts.
Challenge 3 — Productivity at Work

Three shifts define this challenge:

  • From inputs to outputs — the traditional 8-hour/5-day, full separation of work and personal life is giving way to output-oriented models valuing creation over office "face time." Challenges: upholding team cohesion without collocation, mitigating misread non-verbal cues, and assessing output in roles that aren't easily atomizable (e.g., data entry) or quantifiable (e.g., sales).
  • From low- to high-value work — "robotic process automation," from software processing insurance claims to chatbots resolving basic customer issues, eliminates routine tasks so workers can perform higher-value work. Challenges: securing worker buy-in for new processes, placing risky bets on emergent technologies and coding languages, and maintaining customer service quality, especially for non-digital-native customers.
  • From treatment to prevention — facing rising medical costs, employers turn to preventative health coaches and apps for chronic illness and mental health, plus tuition reimbursement and student-loan repayment for retention. Challenges: engaging the high-risk employees most in need, securing leadership buy-in, and assessing ROI on wellness/assistance programs.

The Managerial Framework: 15 Levers Across Three Domains

The note closes with Exhibit 1, a framework of fifteen diagnostic questions organized into three managerial domains — talent management, workplace design, and external collaborations — meant for managers to apply directly to their own organizations.

Talent Management (5 levers)
  1. Assess and continuously train for hard and soft skills — how might assessments, real-time feedback, just-in-time learning, and dedicated training time close capability gaps?
  2. Capture, retain, and codify institutional memory, especially for high-attrition-risk roles — how might redundancies preserve critical expertise and tacit knowledge when workers leave?
  3. Assess and mitigate obstacles preventing workers from considering certain roles — how might marketing, job descriptions, location, hiring processes, and working models be changed to widen discovery and application?
  4. Assess and mitigate obstacles preventing workers from being considered for certain roles — how might sourcing, screening, and evaluation practices separate signal (skills, competencies) from noise (gender, credentials)?
  5. Systematically analyze and mitigate the drivers of absenteeism and turnover — how might employment agreements, working style, benefits, compensation, and promotion policy lower attrition?
Workplace Design (5 levers)
  1. Combat unspoken rules of the workplace that favor "inputs" over "outputs" — how might more explicit performance rubrics level the playing field for workers lacking dominant-culture social capital?
  2. Optimize the firm's monetary and non-monetary value proposition as an employer — how might brand, training, lifestyle, pay, benefits, culture, and values better align with what workers expect?
  3. Assess the extent to which data and technology may improve worker productivity — how might technology improve decisions, augment labor, automate routine tasks, and free workers for higher-value work?
  4. Explore policies that help workers balance work and personal life — how might remote work, vacation, parental, bereavement, and medical leave policies be reformed for sustainable balance?
  5. Expand ways for workers to move between roles and functions for better job fit — how might policy changes ease movement across jobs, functions, and geographies to suit interests and circumstances?
External Collaborations (5 levers)
  1. Develop curriculum design and hiring partnerships with educational institutions — how might co-designed curricula and talent pipelines with regional organizations improve worker quality and supply?
  2. Partner with organizations serving non-traditional pools of talent — how might hiring/retention goals be met through the previously incarcerated, recovered addicts, the physically impaired, and those returning from leave?
  3. Establish industry consortia to pilot, launch, and scale workforce development efforts — how might joint investment by peers, suppliers, and vendors make previously uneconomical training feasible?
  4. Educate and work with lawmakers to improve the availability and quality of workers — how might legislation improve education quality, skilled-talent availability, and worker conditions?
  5. Implement systems to monitor and manage risks arising from outsourcing or offshoring — how might a firm protect its processes, data, reputation, and IP vis-à-vis temporary workers, vendors, and contractors?

Practical implications for this course

This note functions as the course's foundational diagnostic: it operationalizes "talent as a strategic resource" by forcing managers to trace macro forces (technology, demographics, globalization) down to firm-level questions of access, capability, and productivity, and then to a concrete menu of levers — precisely the chain of reasoning Session 1's learning outcomes ask students to practice when linking business strategy to talent acquisition, development, and retention decisions.

Article Session 1

How to Spot — and Develop — High-Potential Talent in Your Organization

Harvard Business Review (Digital Article, Reprint H072D5) · James Intagliata, Jennifer Sturman & Stephen Kincaid (ghSMART) · May 26, 2022

Drawing on a database of more than 23,000 candidate assessments, ghSMART consultants isolate three behavioral markers — Cognitive Quotient (CQ), Drive Quotient (DQ), and Emotional Quotient (EQ) — that predict leadership potential independent of past track record, then show through a Fortune 500 case study how organizations can use the model to widen and diversify their leadership pipeline.

Full breakdown: the CQ/DQ/EQ model, its validation studies and statistics, the "Maya" case study, and the development playbook for each marker.

The problem with using past performance as a proxy for potential

The authors open by naming the core diagnostic failure of most succession pipelines: organizations "look for future leaders by focusing on past track records" — a workable approach only when filling a known role for which candidates have already had the chance to demonstrate the required skills. But past performance "doesn't tell you who can do things they haven't done before," and it systematically underrates high potentials earlier in their careers, especially those "who haven't had equitable access to mentoring, sponsorship, development, and advancement opportunities." This framing positions the model explicitly as a corrective for pipeline inequity, not just a better prediction tool.

Origin and scale of the research base

ghSMART's model is grounded in observable, measurable behaviors rather than achievements. It draws on a database of more than 23,000 candidate assessments for roles at public and private companies, with in-depth analysis of 1,500 individuals ranging from entry-level professionals to senior leaders. From this the authors isolated three psychological markers that reliably predict an individual's ability to grow and handle increased complexity in new roles.

The three markers

  • Cognitive Quotient (CQ) — how a person leverages their intellect.
  • Drive Quotient (DQ) — what motivates them and how they apply their energy.
  • Emotional Quotient (EQ) — how they interact with those around them.

The authors are explicit that these markers do not provide raw measures of intellect, motivation, or interpersonal style the way personality tests or psychometrics attempt to — instead they "capture how people use these qualities on the job," giving organizations "a concrete, objective way to gauge leadership potential, regardless of candidates' depth of experience."

Cognitive Quotient (CQ) — table stakes vs. differentiators

Most organizations already assess intellectual horsepower via academic transcripts, psychometric tests, and case-based interviews — tools the authors flag as "steeped in bias," favoring candidates from elite schools, those who've secured plum early-career positions, and those who know how to "jump through the requisite hoops," privileging book smarts over practical smarts and commercial instinct. The higher-level differentiators to look for instead: does the person routinely step back from their tasks to see things from their manager's (or their manager's manager's) perspective? Do they "look around corners" to anticipate the unexpected? Do they ground even small decisions in how they create value for the business?

Drive Quotient (DQ) — beyond work ethic

The authors distinguish DQ from generic motivation, work ethic, and persistence, which they note are "relatively common among aspiring leaders" and therefore weak differentiators. The real differentiator is how people apply their energy — not only to maximize their own performance but to develop and leverage the capabilities of others, a distinction the authors say is "overlooked in many models." High-DQ individuals push past their comfort zones, attack new challenges "with relish," and are resilient — resetting and reframing after setbacks rather than being derailed by them — while continually striving to amplify results at the organizational level, not just individually.

Emotional Quotient (EQ) — engaging for impact

Companies typically assess EQ only at the basic level: self-awareness, getting along with others, reading the room. The authors argue these are "necessary but not sufficient." The differentiators to search for: people who "engage for impact" — intentionally channeling insight to influence stakeholders and negotiate outcomes — and who are able and willing to deliver difficult messages with both courage and empathy.

Validation studies

Over five years, the authors validated the model empirically in two separate studies:

  • Double-blind study looking only at leaders' earlier career phases: the CQ/DQ/EQ markers correctly differentiated those who later reached the C-suite from those who didn't two times out of three — a rate the authors contrast explicitly against the "50/50 roll of the dice" that typifies conventional hiring and promotion decision accuracy.
  • Multi-year, single-organization study assessing potential for more than 1,800 candidates for key leadership/functional roles, with managers making independent hiring and performance decisions blind to the researchers' ratings. Candidates rated highly on potential were more than three times as likely to be rated top performers by their managers in year one, with the advantage growing (even higher likelihood) through years two, three, and four; highly-rated candidates were also less likely to be involuntarily terminated.

The authors conclude from these results that the model measures "not merely the acquisition of skills but a style of thinking and working that sets the stage for ongoing growth and success."

Case study: developing "Maya," a hidden high potential

A Fortune 500 company engaged ghSMART to identify and develop its high-potential pool, working primarily with leaders two levels below the C-suite. Maya* (name changed for confidentiality) was three levels down and included only when a program spot unexpectedly opened. She was largely unknown to senior leaders, and those who did know her described her — negatively — as "too young," "too eager to please," and as someone who "lacked gravitas."

The ghSMART assessment told a different story across all three markers:

  • CQ: rated highly — she evaluated multiple scenarios rather than locking onto a single answer quickly, and factored in broader industry context and competitive dynamics.
  • DQ: distinguished herself clearly. The daughter of immigrant, blue-collar parents, she chose a college for its financial package over its prestige, excelled there, and — once at the client firm on an accelerated path — consistently sought out stretch assignments from her earliest roles; she also volunteered to lead the company's women's mentorship initiative.
  • EQ: her weakest area. The flip side of her drive to deliver results was underinvestment in relationships: she struggled in situations where facts and data alone couldn't carry her case and persuasion was required, and she tended to route around conflict rather than have tough conversations.

Development actions taken: coaching her to be more intentional about getting to know stakeholders and managing how she "showed up" with them; guidance on tackling difficult issues head-on, with accountability for doing so; and a recommended rotation into a cross-functional assignment to stress-test her collaboration skills. Over the following 24 months, Maya jumped two levels into a high-visibility role where she is thriving; her new boss calls her a "rock star," and she is now at the top of the list for C-suite feeder roles.

Using the model organization-wide

The authors lay out a development playbook once potential is identified:

  • Educate managers on what to look for when recruiting, screening, evaluating, managing performance, and selecting people for development — explicitly teaching that performance alone is not a proxy for potential, and how to recognize CQ/DQ/EQ in people without a track record or whose backgrounds don't match prior generations of leaders.
  • Build "potential profiles" into performance management and talent development processes, with managers assessing CQ/DQ/EQ and coaching employees on developing them.
  • Develop CQ by having people attend senior-leader meetings to gain broader business perspective, assigning cross-unit engagements so employees learn to "connect dots" across functions, and encouraging participation in industry conferences to expose them to issues beyond their own organization.
  • Develop DQ through stretch opportunities — rotating aspiring leaders into new markets or business areas, or giving them larger teams — then observing whether they wait to be told what to learn or proactively seek feedback on it.
  • Develop EQ by starting with organizational culture: spelling out the workplace's "unwritten rules," then tasking people with mapping their stakeholders and making relationship-building an explicit development objective, supported by tools/frameworks for understanding one's own wiring and others' needs.

A closing caveat distinguishes coachability across markers: many behaviors, such as influencing and persuading, are "readily coachable," while others — like thinking more conceptually or strategically — "may be harder to change." The authors advise factoring in how readily any missing behavior can realistically be learned before betting on someone's potential for a new leadership role.

Practical implications for this course

The CQ/DQ/EQ framework gives talent-development strategy an evidence-based alternative to track-record-only succession planning, directly supporting the course objective of designing talent-development systems that surface a "much larger, deeper, more diverse leadership pool than you realized you had" — turning development, not just selection, into a source of sustained competitive advantage.

Article Session 1

The Great Talent Recalibration: How Macroeconomic Shifts Are Reshaping the CHRO Agenda

Bain & Company Insights · Karen Harris, Susan Gunn & John Hazan · Published January 2026

Drawing on Bain's Chief People Officer Forum and a survey of 81 global CPOs, Bain's macro strategists identify four structural forces — post-globalization, capital rationalization, demographic decline, and the decreasing cost of distance — that are converging with AI to redefine the CHRO's mandate from steward of people to architect of the entire 2040 workforce system.

Full breakdown: the CPO survey statistics, the four macro forces reshaping talent, the automation/AI human-interface question, and the expanding CPO mandate.

Framing: an inflection point for HR leadership

The article opens by declaring that organizations are "navigating the most turbulent macroeconomic environment in decades," and that for CPOs, CHROs, and heads of talent, "the ground beneath long-held assumptions is shifting quickly." Post-globalization's effects are evolving, capital is becoming more constrained, labor pools are shrinking, and AI is transforming how work gets done. The authors' central claim, stated directly: "The context in which most HR leaders built their careers is not the context in which they will lead the future." They characterize the present moment as the most tumultuous "since the early 1970s."

The CPO Forum survey

The analysis draws on Bain's survey of 81 CPOs and talent officers across global industries. Headline finding: 70% reported concern about slowing U.S. labor-market momentum and its potential spillover into EMEA and Asia-Pacific economies. A majority of respondents are already considering hiring-footprint adjustments in select markets, with some actively rebalancing where and how they hire globally. The survey also found CPOs prioritizing HR efficiency, AI implementation, and organizational redesign alongside traditional cultural and leadership priorities — evidence, the authors argue, that the CPO mandate is expanding in direct response to the macro environment.

Four structural forces reshaping the global workforce

1. Post-globalization and the new limits on talent mobility

The authors clarify that Bain does not believe the world is "deglobalizing," but that in a "post-global world," friction is growing for trade in goods, services, intellectual property, and capital — including new constraints on the cross-border movement of people, a foundational assumption behind many multinational workforce strategies. The named example: in the semiconductor industry, the U.S. has restricted not just where certain goods may be sold but which passport holders may work on specific technologies destined for certain markets. Visa rules, trade policy, and fast-moving geopolitical shifts — such as tariff changes or staff redeployment following geopolitical conflict — are actively complicating global workforce planning. The takeaway: "talent mobility can no longer be taken for granted," and CPOs must plan for a world where geopolitical boundaries increasingly determine access to skills.

2. Capital scarcity and rising pressure on productivity

The authors describe the end of an era of "capital superabundance": over the last three decades the financial economy grew far faster than the real economy, making capital a lesser constraint than good ideas or great talent. That is reversing — the global system now faces competing capital demands from automation, the energy transition, and large-scale infrastructure development that together exceed annual economic savings, while capital supply itself will grow more slowly than in the prior era. The practical effect for talent executives: growing tension between investing in people versus investing in automation, and greater scrutiny of HR spending and expected ROI — consistent with the survey finding that CPOs are prioritizing HR efficiency and AI implementation.

3. Demographic decline and structural labor shortages

Called "perhaps the most profound and least reversible force shaping the future workforce." Working-age populations in China, Germany, Japan, and other major economies are shrinking. Even in the U.S. — assuming some mitigation of current immigration policy — total workforce growth is projected at only 4% between now and 2050. In China, a country of over a billion people, only 9.5 million babies were born in 2024. The article singles out India as the only large economy with a workforce still growing at scale. The blunt conclusion: companies "will not be able to meet talent needs through hiring alone," making automation "not just a cost lever but a necessity to sustain productivity and economic growth."

4. The decreasing cost of distance and the reconfiguration of work

Technology has enabled greater spatial dispersion in where people live, work, and play. Remote work, which surged during the Covid-19 pandemic, has since stabilized at roughly 20%, while office occupancy remains at about 50% of pre-Covid levels. For CPOs this cuts both ways: hybrid work expands access to talent but challenges apprenticeship, cohesion, and culture, and physical infrastructure "often built for a previous era" must now adapt.

Automation, AI, and the human–machine frontier

The authors treat automation and AI as "a powerful throughline connecting demographics, capital, and globalization." As generative AI and dexterity robotics push automation into service sectors "once considered resistant" — explicitly naming healthcare and food preparation — the central strategic question becomes: "When do we need a human?" The authors note that AI can deliver technically correct answers while still failing at the human interface, offering the relatable example of "anyone who's tried to bypass a chatbot on a service call." The design imperative for workforce planners is therefore not simply to automate tasks but to identify where human presence matters most — where humans provide empathy, judgment, trust, nuance, and the lived experience of navigating complex decisions.

Geopolitics, immigration volatility, and the shifting map of global talent

Recent changes to U.S. working-visa policy are creating anxiety for employees and new administrative burdens for employers, potentially eroding the United States' status as the default destination for global talent. Companies must therefore rethink where their "talent hubs" should be located. The article gives a concrete illustration of how AI is reshaping sourcing decisions: as AI translation reduces language barriers, firms can shift customer-support hiring toward previously overlooked, time-zone-aligned locations — for example, hiring customer support talent in Guatemala instead of the Philippines because of its time-zone alignment with U.S. clients.

The CPO role: from people steward to architect of the 2040 workforce

The authors argue this convergence of forces is redefining what CPOs are asked to do for their CEOs and COOs. They frame the new mandate as three standing questions CPOs must now help answer:

  • In 2040, what is the composition of our workforce?
  • Where do we have people? Where do we have automation?
  • How do we get closest to talent?

The role, in the authors' words, is evolving "from steward of people to strategist of the entire workforce system — including automation, location strategy, and long-term access to skills."

Conclusion: durable, not temporary, forces

The authors close by insisting that the demographic cliff, the shifting rules of globalization, the rising cost of capital, and the rapid acceleration of AI "are not temporary disruptions" but "durable forces that will reshape work for decades." For CPOs, the task ahead is "both challenging and consequential" — leading organizations through a workforce transition that "will define competitive advantage in the years to come."

Practical implications for this course

This article is the course's most direct statement that talent strategy is inseparable from macroeconomic and geopolitical strategy: it gives students a live, 2026 lens for the course's objective of anticipating future talent challenges from technology/AI and shifting workforce expectations, and for treating workforce design — headcount vs. automation, location strategy, skills access — as a board-level strategic decision rather than an HR operating detail.

Session 2 — Recruitment, Bad Hires & Make‑vs‑Buy

Somany Ceramics: Recruitment Challenges

Ivey Publishing, Case W18499 · Amitabh Deo Kodwani, Mukesh Kumar & Sanjeev Prashar · 2018

In December 2016, Hrishikesh Kumar, director of HR at Somany Ceramics Limited (SCL), must decide how to staff a rapidly expanding tile manufacturer in an industry starved of industry-ready technical labour. The case frames the classic "tug vs. train" dilemma — poach trained workers from competitors at a premium, or hire unskilled candidates and train them in-house — as a cost-effectiveness and sustainability problem with no clean answer.

Full digest: company and industry background, competitor profiles, the tug-vs-train dilemma, financials, and the open decision

Company background

SCL began in 1969 as Somany Pilkington, a collaboration with Pilkington Tiles (UK); Indian promoters bought out the UK partner's equity in 1994, and the company was renamed Somany Ceramics Limited in 2007. In 2016 H. L. Somany was chairman emeritus, Shreekant Somany was chairman and managing director, and Abhishek Somany was joint managing director, driving the push for aggressive growth. SCL's stated vision was to be the most sought-after tile and allied-product company in India and "the best employer in the tile industry," pursuing customer delight through business innovation and cost effectiveness. Headquartered in Noida, Uttar Pradesh, SCL manufactured floor and wall tiles, sanitaryware, chrome-plated fittings, and tile-laying solutions (brands/sub-categories include Durastone, GVT/Duragres, Polished Vitrified, Slip Shield, VC Shield, Ezy Fix, Ezy Grout, and Tile Master) from plants in Kassar (Haryana) and Kadi (Gujarat), with combined annual production capacity of 41 million square metres. In 1996 SCL became the first company in India to produce 45cm x 45cm tiles, the largest tile size then made domestically; it earned ISO 9001 (1999) and ISO 14001 (Kadi plant) certifications. In 2016, SCL's market capitalization was approximately ₹31.02 billion (US$462.98 million). The company strongly believed its single differentiator in a crowded, design-driven industry was the quality of its labourers — a workforce of 2,130 staff and workers across both plants (432 staff, 492 permanent workers, 880 contractual workers, 326 casual workers, zero outsourced workers), whose average age fell from 40 (2008-09) to 34 (2014-15), a deliberately youthful profile. Manufacturing capacity at Kassar grew from 21.55 to 25.55 million square metres/year over 2015-16, and subsidiary/associate capacity grew from 20.97 to 25.70 million square metres/year in the same period. SCL sold through a network of 1,490 dealers, 8,000 sub-dealers, and 146 retail showrooms across India, posting FY2015-16 net profit of ₹610.6 million on revenue of ₹17.3 billion.

The ceramic tile industry in India

Globally, the ceramic tile market was valued at $76.81 billion in 2015, forecast to reach $178.1 billion by 2024. India's market was estimated at ₹270 billion ($4.03 billion) as of March 31, 2016, growing at a forecast CAGR of 12-15% versus the global market's 9.8% CAGR — but per-capita consumption in India was only 0.59 square metres, far below Brazil (4.12), China (3.33), and Vietnam (2.8), signalling large headroom. Growth was fuelled by government programs such as Swachh Bharat Abhiyan (Clean India Mission), smart-cities initiatives, and urbanization. India was the third-largest producer and consumer of ceramic tiles globally, per the Indian Council of Ceramic Tiles and Sanitaryware, with quality now matching international standards. Roughly 45-50% of the tiles market remained fragmented among unbranded players. The sector employed over 550,000 people total (50,000 directly, 500,000 indirectly).

Competitors named in the case

Kajaria Ceramics Ltd. — incorporated 1985, India's largest ceramic tile manufacturer, offering 1,200+ wall/floor/designer/vitrified tile options, exporting to 20 countries, with clients including Unitech, DLF, Parsvnath, Sobha, Prestige Group, and Hiranandani Developers; won the Superbrands India title seven times by 2016; FY2015-16 net sales of ₹24.48 billion ($365.37 million). HSIL Ltd. — founded by the Somany family in 1960 (as Hindustan Twyfords Ltd., with UK's Twyfords Ltd.), renamed HSIL in 2009; first Indian building-materials firm to hold ISO 9001, ISO 14001, and OHSAS 18001 (1962); grew via acquisitions of Krishna Ceramics (1989) and Raasi Ceramics (1999); entered tiles in 2010 with the Hindware Italian collection; FY2016 net sales of ₹19.44 billion ($290.19 million). Cera Sanitaryware Ltd. — started 1980, pioneer in colour ranges and water-saving designs, with 400 distributors and 4,000 retailers; tied up with Italy's Rondine Group SpA; tiles contributed only 12% of FY2014-15 turnover versus ~60% from sanitaryware; 2016 revenue of ₹9.34 billion ($139.35 million), up 13.63% YoY, with profit up 23.34% to ₹834.6 million ($12.45 million). Other listed industry players include Roca Sanitaria SA, Asian Granito India Ltd., Nitco Ltd., Orient Bell Ltd., and Pokarna Ltd., among 30 named companies in the case's industry exhibit.

The hiring challenge: "tug or train"

Under Abhishek Somany, SCL wanted to grow faster than its historical pace, requiring technically competent ceramics talent — but the industry faced a chronic shortage of deployment-ready labour, since ceramics technology kept evolving while job seekers showed declining interest in the field. Preference for ceramics careers was low among diploma/degree holders and lower still among graduates; skilled and semi-skilled workers gravitated to IT and construction instead. Of roughly 780,000 engineers entering India's job market annually, 150,000 were IT engineers, and Nielsen's 2012 Campus Track Technology School survey found 63% of 2013 graduating students preferred IT services/products. IT firms would hire fresh graduates of any specialization and train them for 3 months to a year — a model SCL was implicitly weighing against its own build option. IT salaries out-paid manufacturing (entry-level: Software Engineer ₹408,000 vs. Civil/Construction ₹325,000 vs. Ceramics only ₹210,000), and IT jobs also offered superior career paths, work environment, status, and flexibility. Compounding this, only 7-10% of new engineers were judged employable for core engineering jobs at all. Even hired ceramics workers were hard to retain without incremental salary increases (turnover rate ~3%), and traditional talent-acquisition methods had proven ineffective industry-wide.

Kumar's team weighed two options. Option 1 — "tug" (hire from competitors): attracting talent from rivals required paying well above current market rates, which would disturb organizational and industry equilibrium, invite retaliatory poaching of SCL's own skilled staff, and create pay discontent among existing employees. SCL's employee benefit expense had already risen from ₹963.6 million ($15.05 million) in FY2015 to ₹1.12 billion ($16.66 million) in FY2016. Option 2 — "train" (hire and train non-ceramic professionals): shortlist jobless candidates from outside the industry, screen for genuine interest in a ceramics career, and put them through a company-funded training program (SCL paid trainees full salary during training). The risk: candidates might still prove unsuitable after training, sinking the time and cost invested. The majority of Kumar's team favoured the training route, betting that homegrown workers would be easier to retain; a minority worried that identifying and training suitable candidates could itself be slow and costly. The case ends without resolution: SCL's salaries were already in line with the industry average, so the real problem was not pay competitiveness but finding a cost-effective, sustainable five-year workforce strategy — leaving Kumar's decision as the open discussion question.

Course tie-in: Somany's tug-vs-train choice is a textbook make-vs-buy trade-off at the shop-floor level — "buy" (poach) offers speed but inflates labour cost and destabilizes retention, while "make" (train) offers sustainability but carries sunk-cost risk if trainees wash out; the case operationalizes the course's make-buy-borrow-build framework in a labour-scarce manufacturing context rather than a white-collar one.

What to Do When You Realize You Made a Bad Hire

Harvard Business Review, Digital Article, Reprint H04HJN · Liz Kislik · August 22, 2018

Kislik offers a manager's playbook for the moment a promising hire turns into a liability: confront the problem directly, attempt repair through feedback or reassignment, weigh the true cost of keeping the person against the cost of replacing them, and — if needed — execute a respectful, well-managed exit. Only 19% of new hires are considered fully successful, and by 18 months 46% are deemed failures.

Full digest: the five-step playbook, client vignettes, and the cost-diagnosis framework

Opening statistic and framing

Kislik opens by citing "a frequently cited study": only 19% of new hires are considered fully successful, and by the 18-month mark, 46% are deemed failures. The core dilemma facing a manager who suspects a bad hire is whether it's worse to keep an employee who can't handle the work and is damaging the team, or to publicly admit a significant hiring mistake. Her thesis: it is usually less costly to make a change, and the sooner the change is made, the better.

Step 1 — Prepare for a direct, uncomfortable conversation

Rather than hoping for the best or avoiding confrontation, managers should level with the new hire about performance concerns and invite joint problem-solving. Suggested opener: "James, I want to talk about the last few weeks, and where we seem to be on track and where we need to make some adjustments." The new employee may already sense the same problems and welcome the chance to address them collaboratively.

Step 2 — Try to repair through focused feedback or reassignment

Vignette 1: a client hired a junior staffer for his "moxie, energy, and drive," but within weeks he was voicing dissatisfaction, making careless mistakes, and goofing around. His manager gave careful feedback, asked why the job felt unsatisfying, and — helped by business growth and organizational flexibility — reassigned him to a more challenging role in another department, supported by an intensive training program; the transition succeeded. Vignette 2 (repair failing): a new vice president hired from a different industry made repeated commitments to apply feedback but could not adapt her technical skills to the business model. Kislik flags the risk of escalation of commitment — the tendency to resist "giving up" on a tough situation — and advises that if significant personal effort and real improvement aren't visible within roughly three to six months, it's time to cut losses.

Step 3 — Identify the current and future expense of keeping the bad hire

Vignette 3: a senior executive from a very large public company joined a midsize family-owned client with unrealistic expectations about resources and autonomy, costing the business dearly; neither feedback nor reassignment could fix this. Kislik enumerates the typical costs of retaining a bad hire: reduced productivity or increased opportunity costs, employee disengagement and possible turnover of others, and increased interdepartmental conflict. Clear indicators include missed deadlines and declining work quality; a subtler signal is extra pre- and post-meeting meetings, often colleagues quietly compensating for or working around the underperformer. These costs should be weighed against the cost of replacement and onboarding a new candidate. Vignette 4: at one client, the true scale of damage from an underperforming executive — bottlenecked work, suppressed innovation, colleague dissension — was only visible in hindsight, after her removal, when others stepped up to tackle previously stalled initiatives.

Step 4 — Make the case for an exception to the typical exit plan

If the relationship can't be salvaged, managers should seek the smoothest, most graceful transition possible. Where feasible, negotiate a mutually beneficial exit — offering the departing employee a sense of control while giving the manager latitude to support the team publicly and search for a replacement. Suggested opener: "I appreciate your telling me how concerned you are, and the current situation is having a negative impact on the team, too, so I wonder if we can work this out in a way that benefits everyone." Kislik stresses checking with HR before pursuing this route. If a surprise termination is unavoidable, be direct: "As we've discussed several times, someone in your role needs to be able to accomplish these tasks and goals successfully, and you haven't been able to do that. So unfortunately, we're going to have to terminate your employment as of such-and-such date."

Step 5 — Offer severance/outplacement and learn from the mistake

Offering severance and outplacement services signals good faith to both the departing employee and their colleagues, even though most companies reserve severance for long-tenured staff — taking responsibility for a hiring mistake speeds everyone's ability to move on. The exception: cases where the employee misrepresented their skills or exhibited ethical/behavioral problems. Finally, managers should diagnose what went wrong to avoid repeating it, since the replacement hire's success is now critical. Moving deliberately but quickly preserves the departing employee's other job prospects and improves the odds of still having a viable slate of candidates to draw from.

Course tie-in: Kislik's cost enumeration — lost productivity, disengagement-driven turnover of others, conflict, and the "extra meetings" tax — is a direct, practitioner-level answer to the ILO on quantifying the cost of poor talent decisions across the lifecycle, and complements Cappelli's more analytical mismatch-cost framing by showing what those costs look like day to day.

Opportunities and Risks of Artificial Intelligence in Recruitment and Selection

International Journal of Organizational Analysis · Olajide Ore & Martin Sposato · 2022

A qualitative study of ten professional recruiters at a Fortune 500 multinational manufacturer finds that AI is welcomed for automating routine recruitment tasks — data analytics, candidate experience, and employer branding — but simultaneously provokes fear and distrust rooted in concerns about bias, privacy, reliability, and the erosion of the "human touch." The authors conclude recruiters must become AI advocates rather than resist the technology, while organizations build in checks against algorithmic bias.

Full digest: methodology, the three opportunity themes, the two risk themes, cited literature, and conclusions

Purpose, methodology and sample

The study explores recruiter perspectives on AI's opportunities and risks in recruitment and selection via face-to-face, semi-structured in-depth interviews with ten professional recruiters (REC1-REC10) at an unnamed multinational corporation (MNC) — a Fortune 500 manufacturer with more than 90,000 employees globally, corporate offices in 70 countries, and manufacturing facilities in 200 countries; 50% of global headcount and all senior HR leadership were U.S. nationals. Participants' job tenure ranged 1-11 years (average 6, SD 2), and recruitment/selection experience ranged 4-22 years (average 10, SD 4). Analysis followed Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase thematic analysis, with interviews transcribed verbatim and coded into themes tied to three research questions about AI opportunities/risks in the recruitment function.

Opportunity theme 1 — Data analytics

AI/ML technologies applied to HR data ("HR analytics," "workforce analytics," "people analytics") were seen as enabling better, faster hiring decisions by modelling historical applicant data to automate screening and candidate ranking. The paper names vendors Ideal, Arya, and Talent Genie as offering AI tools that trawl job boards, social media, and professional networks to source even passive candidates. Recruiter quotes: "Maybe, with AI automatically screening the CVs in the system and then saying that this is the best-qualified person for the job, you get a better hiring decision" (REC2); "it will help with the assessment part, and we can use the system to make more data-driven decisions" (REC4); "if AI could mine the information in the system and recognise the required keywords and then rank the best candidates for me, that would be ideal" (REC5); "a system that could pre-screen candidates based on their answers and then automatically send feedback too, it would be very useful" (REC8).

Opportunity theme 2 — Candidate experience

AI-freed time was seen as enabling more responsive, timely engagement with applicants, addressing a known e-recruitment problem: high application volumes limit recruiters' capacity for open communication, and rejected candidates who receive no prompt feedback can spread negative word-of-mouth. Quotes: "We would really be able to focus on the candidate experience by making use of data analytics... to provide candidates with a first-class experience" (REC6); "I think you could improve the candidate experience... if you can do something quicker and make it more fun" (REC9); "AI will allow us to take advantage of the free time that we will have so that we can improve the candidate experience" (REC10).

Opportunity theme 3 — Employer branding

Recruiters saw AI-driven efficiencies as freeing capacity to invest in employer brand and expand the applicant pool. Quotes: "AI includes the opportunity to improve our employer branding so that we can present some of the good things that the company represents" (REC1); "The war for talent will be to focus on attracting candidates in terms of what our company can offer to them and the kind of culture that we have" (REC3); "AI would really allow us to have an opportunity to positively market our core company values to attract candidates" (REC7).

Risk theme 1 — Fear

Recruiters voiced fear about AI's reliability, accuracy, data privacy, discrimination potential, and the loss of human interaction (or human recruiters altogether). Quotes: "I am worried because we don't know what the limit of AI is, so there will be challenges around the privacy of people's personal data... people have the right to have their private lives" (REC2); "there will be some anxiety about the replacement of human recruiters" (REC3); "the loss of human interaction in the process is something that the MNC and the wider society should really be concerned about" (REC4); "We could face ethical issues because of discrimination, mainly because I also think AI has its own potential for bias" (REC7); "the fear of letting computers take over and humans not having anything to do with it" (REC8); "data protection and privacy will be a problem because AI will have access to a lot of personal information that may be used to make discriminatory decisions" (REC9).

Risk theme 2 — Distrust

Distrust centred on whether algorithmic decisions could ever be bias-free, given that models learn from historical, potentially biased organizational data. Quotes: "If AI is not reliable, then I would not have the faith to rely on it solely as the only option to discover the best candidates" (REC1); "if the person who is feeding information to the AI is discriminating against certain people or groups of people, then AI would do the same" (REC6); "human error can be built into the algorithm... you would want to have something that's validated" (REC5); "There's no standard way to write a CV, so potentially good candidates may not be screened correctly if they don't have the right keywords" (REC10).

Literature cited on bias and discrimination risk

The authors note Cappelli (2001, p. 144) concluded AI-based selection technology could potentially "reject a disproportionate number of underrepresented groups, including women, minority ethnics, disabled people and workers older than forty years of age." Hurlburt (2017) warned of coders deliberately or inadvertently embedding cultural/personal bias into algorithms, asking "How could AI be trusted to know that the algorithms codes were free from their own biases?" Housman (2016), by contrast, argued algorithmic selection could actually reduce bias relative to human recruiters' tendency to hire people similar to themselves. Scholz (2017) cautioned that AI-based recruitment poses fairness/discrimination risks that could alienate candidates. The unaddressed-bias risk carries potential legal exposure: discrimination lawsuits and reputational/brand damage plus regulatory fines.

Conclusions and practical implications

AI promises to mitigate bias but could equally exacerbate it by encoding human bias into machine-learning algorithms trained on historical data. AI's clearest value is automating mundane sourcing/screening so recruiters can focus on strategy, candidate experience, and employer branding. Fear and distrust stem from perceived limits in AI's accuracy, reliability, and the risk of recruiters being displaced — though participants uniformly believed human interaction, empathy, and cultural-fit judgment will always be needed in recruitment. The authors recommend recruiters become "AI advocates," treating the technology as a tool to improve performance rather than a threat, while organizations avoid a "blind faith" approach and build in checks and balances to ensure algorithmic integrity.

Course tie-in: AI adoption in recruitment functions as a cost-and-risk lever within the make-vs-buy lifecycle — it can lower the cost of sourcing and screening (the "buy" side of talent acquisition) but, if ungoverned, raises the tail-risk cost of a bad hire by encoding bias into the very screening step meant to prevent one.

The Make-Versus-Buy Decision: Deciding Whether to Hire Internally or From the Outside

HBS Press Chapters (excerpted from Talent on Demand: Managing Talent in an Age of Uncertainty) · Peter Cappelli · 2008

Cappelli borrows operations-research logic — the "news agent problem" of estimating uncertain demand — to argue that neither pure internal development ("make") nor pure outside hiring ("buy") solves modern talent management; the right approach blends both based on how long talent is needed, how predictable demand is, and the asymmetric costs of overshooting versus undershooting the talent forecast.

Full digest: the rise and limits of outside hiring, the IT industry cautionary tale, mismatch-cost theory, real options, and the prescriptive decision framework

Framing analogy: talent management as supply chain

Cappelli opens with a domestic anecdote (borrowing printer paper from his wife) to illustrate that failing to manage talent proactively is equivalent to failing to manage a supply chain — the cost of an unplanned response seems small until an employee quits or retires without warning, at which point client relationships, project continuity, and productivity can suffer materially. The traditional internal fix — "Do we have anyone here who can take over?" — creates bumping chains: filling one vacancy internally creates a new vacancy elsewhere. Cappelli cites Diane Rothbard Margolis's 1970s account of corporate life in which one executive reported that a single vacancy triggered five additional job changes. Each internal move risks placing someone in a role they're unprepared for, hurting performance and morale.

The rise and limits of outside hiring

Outside ("buy") hiring became dominant from the mid-1980s because it was radically simpler than internal development: managers could be hired "just in time" for vacancies, with no need to fund training or maintain developmental career ladders — prior employers had already borne those costs as sunk costs, so the hiring firm paid only the market wage. But this created an incomplete contract problem: firms that invested in developing employees risked losing them to non-training competitors who could pay more, and once employers began breaking the implicit "develop-and-retain" bargain via layoffs, employees felt equally free to leave when it benefited them.

Cautionary case study: the IT industry

Cappelli traces IT's shift from traditional internal-development firms (IBM, Hewlett-Packard, Sperry, Univac, and Route 128 firms like Wang, Data General, Digital Equipment Corporation) to the Silicon Valley "buy" model that recruited talent away from those larger firms with stock options and exciting projects — and ultimately beat the Route 128 companies in the market. But the just-in-time model left firms at the mercy of a volatile external labour supply. Real wages for IT graduates peaked in 1986, then fell amid the 1991 recession; IT-degree enrolment declined through the mid-1990s in response, then rebounded sharply (+40% in 1996-97, +39% in 1997-98) just as the market overheated, followed by the 2001 tech-market collapse that flooded the market with new graduates right as demand cratered — a textbook demonstration of supply lagging demand signals. Only about half of IT professionals held bachelor's degrees in an IT-related field at the height of the boom, and only ~10% of programmers held any bachelor's degree; non-degree credentialing bodies like Cisco Systems and Microsoft issued more than two million credentials. Software engineer turnover averaged roughly 40% per year. Career persistence data: only 19% of computer science graduates remained in that field twenty years later, versus 52% of civil engineering graduates; 36% of all programming graduates had left the field entirely; a George Mason University study found IT workers were twice as likely to have changed careers as workers in other occupations. Age bias compounded the problem: an Information Week survey found only 2% of IT managers would hire someone with 10+ years' experience, and a Network World survey found only 13% of 20-30 year-olds would hire anyone over 40; among laid-off electronics/electrical engineers, only 4% found re-employment easy, and 76% cited age as a significant hindrance.

The pendulum swings back

By the end of the 1990s, many employers outside IT concluded pure outside hiring wasn't working and began rebuilding internal talent pipelines. Example: London-based Co-Operative Group (50,000 employees) had filled up to 80% of executive vacancies via outside search firms; CEO Martin Beaumont reversed course, targeting 70% internal appointments. Canadian HR observers describe a parallel pattern of firms returning to internal development after decades of "hands-off," just-in-time hiring.

The talent inventory problem and mismatch costs

Cappelli reframes talent planning using the operations-research "news agent problem" — a newsstand operator must estimate tomorrow's demand, balancing the cost of too few papers (lost sales) against too many (wasted inventory). Applied to talent, undersupply and oversupply are collectively "mismatch costs." Critically, these costs are not symmetric: the cost of undershooting talent demand has fallen because outside hiring can fill gaps quickly, whereas the cost of overshooting — carrying excess "bench" talent — has risen, because underused, high-potential employees sitting idle are prime flight risks (any competitor offering immediate use of their skills can lure them away) and because "excess" talent looks like "fat" to cost-cutting restructurers. Evidence: a 2006 Towers Perrin global survey of 86,000 employees found 15% were actively trying to leave their employer and 43% were open to other opportunities. Watson-Wyatt research found recent training recipients were paradoxically more likely to leave (presumably poached by competitors after being upskilled). Wharton students surveyed said they would wait an average of only ten months for a promotion before concluding their advancement was blocked. Executive quotes: Scott MacMeekin (CEO, Bossard Trans-Pacific) — "if there is a guy good enough to be running the company effectively, they would resign... If he is capable, someone will pick him off." Peter Johnson (CEO, Inchcape plc) — "You develop them over a two to three year period. They're ready for the bigger job. The bigger job isn't available, then you lose them, and very often they go to your competition." A boxed vignette on General Electric notes GE is famous for developing talent internally — seven former GE HR executives went on to head HR elsewhere between 1998 and 2000 — illustrating that a deep bench can represent both a competitive strength and an "excess inventory" of talent developed partly for competitors' benefit.

The economics of management oversupply (Figure 4-1 and the Hay System)

Cappelli models an employee's value-to-cost ratio over a career: early on, cost (driven by market entry wages) exceeds value produced; a middle period (roughly years 3-5 onward) is when the employer recoups its developmental investment as value outpaces compensation growth (fixed by internal job-evaluation systems); late-career, seniority-based pay again exceeds tapering performance. The Hay Company's "Hay System" (assigning "Hay points" to jobs) was the classic tool used to manage this compensation architecture under the "Organization Man" model. This system broke down in the 1980s when outside hiring let new employers pay trained candidates more than their original employer (who needed to recoup training costs), forcing training employers to choose between raising wages (eroding ROI on development) or losing talent. Executive pay inflation compounded the erosion: average U.S. CEO compensation rose from $1.3 million in 1993 to $2.5 million in 2003 (7.1%/year, more than double general wage growth), with other senior executives earning roughly 40% of CEO pay and rising at a similar rate — an additional ~$1 million (net of inflation) moving to the CEO annually versus a decade earlier, plus ~$400,000 per other executive, all reducing the net return on executive development investment.

The make-or-buy framework: four diagnostic questions

Cappelli explicitly rejects "make versus buy" as an either/or choice — "the better option is to do some of both." He offers four questions to determine the mix: (1) How long will you need the talent? Longer need favours internal development, since it's easier to recoup investment. (2) Is there a hierarchy of skills/jobs enabling candidates to learn progressively (more likely within functional areas)? If so, internal development is easier. (3) How important is preserving the organization's current culture? Outside hires import different norms and can deliberately or unpredictably change culture — cited example: the nuclear power industry, where the Nuclear Regulatory Commission has directed firms with safety problems to hire new managers, often from the Navy (noted for its safety record), specifically to change culture. (4) How accurate is your demand forecast? Less certainty raises the risk/cost of internal development. Illustrative example: IBM found itself with too many candidates in its high-potential program relative to available roles, and cut the program's size in half to make room for more outside hires and fresh thinking. A boxed example, "The Mismatch Problem at Unilever India," describes Unilever's historically strong internal-development culture (three candidates competing for each promotion; ~half of each management-trainee cohort left within ten years) unraveling post-2000 as restructuring flattened the pyramid and slowed advancement — yet high relative pay kept managers from quitting, so headcount in one division rose from 1,100 (2000) to 1,400 (2004) even as managerial demand fell; Unilever ultimately resolved the overhang by transferring surplus managers to international operations rather than laying them off.

Beyond binary hiring: outsourcing and leasing as risk transfer

Outside options extend beyond direct hiring to leasing employees via staffing agencies or outsourcing work to independent providers entirely. Outsourcing lets a firm precisely bound the work and shift the risk of securing needed talent to the contractor — but the client still pays a price for that risk transfer through contract premiums, especially for shorter (more accurate but riskier-to-the-contractor) contract terms. Cappelli's blunt conclusion: "When it comes to managing the risks associated with talent management, outsourcing offers no free lunch for employers."

Estimating mismatch costs in practice

Cappelli proposes practical diagnostic questions for planning teams: what are the odds actual demand falls within 10% of the forecast either way; what does a 10% shortfall cost (via outside hiring or alternatives) versus a 10% surplus (idle time, retention risk); and repeating the exercise for a 50% miss in either direction. Because overshoot costs (lost investment, retention risk, restructuring exposure) typically exceed undershoot costs (correctable via outside hiring), Cappelli recommends deliberately developing *less* talent internally than the point-estimate forecast and planning to fill the gap externally. Illustrative example: if the forecast calls for 100 programmers and the estimate is judged fairly reliable, develop ~90 internally and plan to hire ~10 externally; if the estimate is more like a guess, develop only ~60 and hire the rest. A named real-world case: Vivek Gupta, senior vice president at Indian software firm Zensar Technologies, described the company initially undershooting its internal-development target and filling gaps externally — until the labour market tightened, making external fill-ins harder and shifting the balance of costs, at which point Zensar increased internal development and began deliberately overshooting its point estimate to buffer against attrition.

Real options for talent decisions

Borrowing the finance concept of real options, Cappelli frames questions like "should we keep two recruiters on the payroll despite no current hiring need?" as option-value problems. Worked example: retaining two recruiters costs $100,000/year; if there's a 10% chance business rebounds enough to need them, and doing so quickly (versus starting from scratch) would save $4,200,000 against an incremental spend of $1,000,000, the net benefit if the option is exercised is $3,200,000; weighted by the 10% probability, the expected value is $320,000 — comfortably justifying the $100,000 retention cost.

Course tie-in: This is the course's core make-vs-buy text — Cappelli's four diagnostic questions (duration of need, skill hierarchy, culture fit, forecast accuracy) operationalize the ILO to "evaluate internal vs. external talent strategies," and his asymmetric mismatch-cost logic gives a quantifiable language for the ILO on evaluating short-term vs. long-term talent trade-offs and the cost of poor talent decisions across the lifecycle.

Future of Jobs Report 2025

World Economic Forum · January 2025

The WEF's flagship labour-market survey of over 1,000 employers across 55 economies and 22 industry clusters projects that technological change, geoeconomic fragmentation, the green transition, demographic shifts and economic uncertainty will jointly create 170 million jobs and displace 92 million by 2030 — a structural churn equal to 22% of today's 1.2 billion formal jobs. It maps which specific roles and skills are rising and falling fastest, and identifies skills gaps as the number-one barrier employers face in transforming their organizations.

Headline employment-churn figures

By 2030, macrotrend-driven job creation is estimated at 170 million jobs (14% of today's total employment), offset by displacement of 92 million current jobs (8% of total employment), netting a 78 million-job increase (7% of today's total employment). This represents a structural labour-market churn of 22% of the 1.2 billion formal jobs in the dataset studied — figures drawn jointly from the WEF's Future of Jobs Survey 2024 and ILO ILOSTAT data.

Leading the fastest-growing roles by net percentage growth are Big Data Specialists, FinTech Engineers, AI and Machine Learning Specialists, and Software and Applications Developers, followed by Security Management Specialists, Data Warehousing Specialists, Autonomous and Electric Vehicle Specialists, UI and UX Designers, Light Truck or Delivery Services Drivers, Internet of Things Specialists, Data Analysts and Scientists, Environmental Engineers, Information Security Analysts, DevOps Engineers, and Renewable Energy Engineers. Broadening digital access is the single most divergent driver of labour-market change, expected to create 19 million jobs while displacing 9 million — more of both than any other macrotrend. AI and information-processing technology trends are projected to create 11 million jobs and displace 9 million, the largest bidirectional impact of any technology trend specifically. Elsewhere, geoeconomic fragmentation is implicated in 7 million jobs of disruption (the 5th-largest macrotrend effect), and rising cost of living is projected to drive more job destruction (3 million) than creation (2 million), while a separate demographic trend is linked to creation of 4 million jobs and displacement of 3 million.

Analytical thinking remains the single most sought-after core skill, rated essential by 7 in 10 companies, followed by resilience/flexibility/agility and leadership and social influence. AI and big data top the list of fastest-growing skills, followed closely by networks and cybersecurity, then technological literacy; creative thinking, resilience/flexibility/agility, and curiosity/lifelong learning round out the top rising skills, alongside leadership and social influence, talent management, analytical thinking, and environmental stewardship. In the top 10 industries surveyed, over 90% of respondents expect AI and big data skill demand to increase. At the other end, manual dexterity, endurance and precision shows the most pronounced net decline — 24% of respondents foresee decreasing importance, the first time this category of physical skill has registered a net negative trend in the report's history — alongside milder declines in reading/writing/mathematics and dependability/attention to detail.

The report frames workforce transformation need starkly: "if the world's workforce was made up of 100 people, 59 would need training by 2030." Predictive HR Analytics and Talent Management: A Conceptual Framework

Journal of Management Analytics · R. Navodya Gurusinghe, Bhadra J. H. Arachchige & Dushar Dayarathna · 2021

This conceptual paper builds the first theoretical model linking predictive HR analytics (PHRA) capability to talent management outcomes, fusing the Technology-Organisation-Environment (TOE) framework with Resource-Based Theory (RBT) and identifying data-driven culture as the moderating variable that converts basic HR analytics adoption into a genuine predictive capability. It proposes seven testable propositions spanning technological, organisational and environmental antecedents through to talent acquisition, development and retention outcomes.

Full digest: theoretical lenses, all antecedent constructs and propositions, Bersin's four-level HR analytics maturity model, data-driven culture as moderator, talent management outcomes, and research/practice implications including regulatory/ethical data-use considerations.

Research gap and objective

The authors note that despite 71% of companies believing HR analytics is a pressing concern (Deloitte University Press, 2017), only 8% report having usable data and only 9% believe they have clear insight into which talent elements drive firm performance; just 15% have implemented HR/talent scorecards for line managers. The paper's stated objective is to answer three research questions: (1) what contextual factors drive HR analytics adoption, (2) how these factors build PHRA capability under the moderating effect of data-driven culture, and (3) how PHRA capability affects talent management outcomes. The authors state this is "possibly the first study to propose a theoretical model to examine the effect of PHRA capability on talent management outcomes."

Theoretical lenses

Technology-Organisation-Environment (TOE) framework (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990): explains innovation adoption via three contextual elements — technological context (available/in-use technologies), organisational context (firm size, structure, resources, employee skills, communication processes), and environmental context (industry competition, value-chain partners, regulatory environment). It is consistent with Rogers's (1983) Diffusion of Innovation (DOI) theory.

Resource-Based Theory (RBT), rooted in Penrose (1959) and formalized by Wernerfelt (1984) and Barney (1991): argues that valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable (VRIN) resources/capabilities generate sustainable competitive advantage. The paper "fuses" TOE and RBT because TOE alone does not explain how HR analytics becomes a predictive capability or improves talent outcomes, while RBT alone omits external/environmental factors — critical given HR analytics deals with sensitive people-data subject to varying national regulation.

Full conceptual model: antecedents → adoption → PHRA capability → outcomes

Technological antecedent — Technology competence (Proposition 1: positively related to HR analytics adoption): combines technology infrastructure and IT human resources (Zhu & Kraemer, 2005). Citing Kaur and Fink's (2017) 22-interview, 16-organisation study, the most-used HR analytics tools are R (94% of interviewed companies), Tableau (83%), Python (50%), SPSS (44%) and Excel (44%); named AI-enabled HR analytics tools include IBM's Watson Talent, Intel's Saffron, and HireVue's video-analytics platform. Key impediments: data not gathered or inaccurate, data siloed across functions/geographies, and basic reports built on inefficiency-based metrics.

Organisational antecedents:

  • Basic resources (Proposition 2a) — financial investment and time, since HR analytics initiatives may not yield immediate ROI.
  • Analytical competencies of HR personnel (Proposition 2b) — the ability to develop causal logic, apply appropriate analytics, ensure proper measures, and embed insights into decision-making (Kryscynski et al., 2017); the paper notes most HR personnel are not trained data scientists, yet must understand the process well enough to avoid wrong conclusions and to defend findings when they conflict with senior executives' instincts.
  • Top management commitment (Proposition 2c) — C-suite support is harder to secure in HR specifically because the function is seen as transactional/administrative rather than profit-driving; HR must build credibility among managers who distrust data-driven results.
  • HR-Business strategic alignment (Proposition 2d) — analytics projects must be explicitly linked to business strategy and deliverables, not just activities, requiring HR to understand the firm's value chain beyond the HR function.

Environmental antecedents:

  • Regulatory support (Proposition 3a) — regulatory environments vary sharply by country: in the US, employee data collected is treated as company property; EU organisations must justify why they collect personal employee data; monitoring employees' private emails is illegitimate in Canada. The paper argues regulatory clarity on how organisations may collect, analyse and act on people-data is a precondition for responsible adoption.
  • Competitive pressure (Proposition 3b) — firms adopt HR analytics under pressure when peers use it to manage talent, particularly in industries facing talent shortages.

Moderator — Data-driven culture (Proposition 4): defined via Gupta and George (2016) as "the extent to which organisational members (including top-level executives, middle managers, and lower-level employees) make decisions based on the insights extracted from data." The paper argues that no matter how sophisticated the analytics software, if leadership continues deciding by gut feeling, the firm cannot progress from basic HR analytics adoption to true predictive (PHRA) capability.

Outcomes — Talent management (Propositions 5a-5c): PHRA capability is proposed as positively related to all three elements of the talent lifecycle — talent acquisition, talent development, and talent retention (per Lewis & Heckman, 2006 and Tarique & Schuler, 2010). The paper explicitly cites Walford-Wright and Scott-Jackson's (2018) "Talent Rising" model as an example of significant improvement in talent-acquisition metrics following implementation of a technology-driven analytics stack.

Bersin's four-level HR analytics maturity model

Cited to frame PHRA as the top of a maturity ladder: Level 1, Operational reporting (56% of organisations in 2016) — reactive headcount/turnover/absenteeism metrics from siloed systems; Level 2, Advanced reporting (30%) — proactive, descriptive reporting embedded in daily business; Level 3, Advanced analytics (10%) — statistical models supporting proactive problem identification; Level 4, Predictive analytics — scenario planning and forecasting (e.g., identifying future talent shortages, high-potential talent, and critical positions), typically requiring dedicated data scientists and tools like R, and associated with firms having a CHRO on the board or a people-analytics centre of excellence reporting directly to the CEO.

Key statistic on skills concern

The paper cites PricewaterhouseCoopers (2020): 74% of global CEOs report that "a lack of availability of the right skills is a concern."

Implications for practice and ethics/regulatory considerations

The paper's practical implications target management, HR practitioners, talent managers, IT managers and HR analytics software vendors: firms lacking dedicated HR analytics software can still build competence using advanced Excel; managers can build C-suite buy-in by demonstrating outcomes from pilot HR analytics projects; and software vendors can tailor value propositions around the technological, organisational and environmental factors identified. On governance, the paper stresses that legal and ethical boundaries around collecting, analysing, and acting on people-related data must be defined before implementing HR analytics, since national regulatory regimes differ substantially in what employee data may be collected and how consent must be obtained.

Gurusinghe, Arachchige & Dayarathna (2021): "Predictive HR analytics is a strategic capability that organisation can build using firm resources and skills under the effect of firm's data-driven culture."
Deloitte University Press (2017), cited in Gurusinghe et al.: "71% of companies believe in HR analytics as a pressing concern in their firms. Nevertheless, only very few companies (8%) reported that they have usable data."

Practical implications for TS-ADR

This is the course's most direct theoretical scaffold for "interpreting talent dashboards": Bersin's four-level maturity model gives students a diagnostic lens for where an organisation's own dashboards sit (operational reporting vs. true predictive capability), while the paper's regulatory-support proposition is a direct entry point for AI bias risk modelling — since predictive models built on historical people-data can encode and amplify past discriminatory patterns unless regulatory and data-quality safeguards (data completeness, accuracy, and clearly defined consent boundaries) are built in before scaling PHRA capability.

Talent Rising: People Analytics and Technology Driving Talent Acquisition Strategy

Strategic HR Review · Gavin Walford-Wright & William Scott-Jackson · 2018

Drawing on qualitative and quantitative research across multiple organisations, this paper diagnoses the strategic vacuum in typical talent-acquisition functions and proposes "Talent Rising" — a modular, plug-and-play technology stack combining four selection tools — built around three objectives: reduce time to hire, reduce cost per hire, and increase quality of hire. Implementation is reported to produce significant improvement across all key talent-acquisition metrics, while the paper closes with an explicit warning against uncritical "technology lust."

Full digest: diagnosed starting-condition problems, the Talent Rising process architecture and four selection tools, worker-to-retiree ratio and other named statistics, named companies and quotes, and the closing caution against technology lust.

Three core objectives

The Talent Rising model is built around three strategic objectives mapped explicitly onto Porter's (1985) three generic competitive strategies: reduce time to hire (innovation — rewiring existing processes into lean, technology-enabled workflows), reduce cost per hire (cost leadership — building internal capability and technology versus reliance on external recruitment agencies), and increase quality of hire (quality — measured via engagement, retention and performance).

Diagnosed starting-condition problems

Based on meetings with talent-acquisition teams globally and internal survey reviews, the authors identify a recurring lack of core strategic components in many organisations: no clear vision; no applicant tracking system (ATS); low engagement; no people analytics; no referral programme; high cost per hire (from reliance on external agencies); long time to hire (from lack of automation and manual handoffs); lower quality of hire (from poor or absent assessments); poor relationships/lack of trust with hiring managers and internal applicants; and no KPIs or individual development plans (IDPs) for the talent-acquisition team itself. The authors conclude that a lack of technology and poor process design directly produces both candidate and internal-client dissatisfaction.

Market context and the worker-to-retiree ratio

Citing the 2015 McKinsey Global Growth Model study (covering 2005-2015), the paper notes there were three times as many workers as retirees at that point, but that within the next decade this ratio will fall to 1:1 — intensifying competition for scarce, specialised talent, particularly in technology and life sciences. The paper also cites Gartner's 2014 prediction that the Big Data and analytics market would generate US$3.7 trillion in products and services and over 4.4 million new jobs by 2015. Separately, CIPD (2015) research found that a quarter of organisations reported technology was already replacing some jobs within their organisation.

People analytics and named companies

The paper notes that "less than half of HR leaders believe that they link their data to key business and financial data," despite people analytics now being a "must-have" HR capability. Citing Davenport et al. (2010), the authors note that as early as 2010, leading companies including Google, Best Buy, P&G and Sysco were already using sophisticated data collection and analysis to remove guesswork from talent management. Google is discussed at length: Laszlo Bock's (then SVP of People Operations) principle that hiring "doesn't matter who you are, where you're from or what school you went to" is used to argue for actively countering human bias by seeking non-traditional applicants; Google's dedicated "people analytics" HR division measures qualities such as social skills, flexibility, emotional intelligence, initiative, attitude, and perseverance; and Google's Director of People Analytics, Dr. Brian Welle, is quoted on the business case for workforce diversity.

The "Talent Rising" process architecture and four selection tools

Following extensive literature review and field implementation across several organisations, four selection tools were identified as the architecture's core components, each mapped to a specific stage of the hiring funnel: an organisational culture-fit tool (for sifting), cognitive ability assessments (for sifting), video interviewing tools (for screening), and personality tools (for selecting). These evaluate candidates against four key questions: does the person fit organisational culture; do they have potential to learn quickly and grow; do they have the experience/knowledge to operate at the necessary level of independence; and do they behave in line with role competencies. The model is explicitly designed as an "a la carte," plug-and-play tech stack, adoptable modularly depending on an organisation's requirements and investment appetite, and intended for use by organisations, academics and policy makers alike.

The paper also discusses newer candidate-experience innovations, including candidate concierge services — mobile apps offering GPS guidance to interview locations, background information on interviewers, and geo-location beacon notifications as candidates cross campus landmarks — alongside special tours and culture presentations for candidates.

Predictive/template-building value of people analytics

Beyond selection, the paper frames talent technology's greatest HR value as predictive: by analysing the skills and attributes of both high and low performers, organisations can build a "template" for future hires, enabling a competency and behavioural assessment that screens out likely-unsuccessful candidates while ranking those with potential. The resulting "3D" view of a candidate (visual, audio, and assessment-derived factual results) is described as democratic, impartial and meritocratic, expanding the addressable hiring pool beyond geographically-restricted active candidates to passive candidates sourced via platforms like LinkedIn and Facebook.

Results

Implementation of the Talent Rising model is reported to produce a significant improvement in all key metrics related to the talent-acquisition process. The role of the recruiter and talent-acquisition team is described as having radically changed, evolving into an internal strategic partner and organisational advisor rather than a transactional gatekeeper.

Closing caution: "technology lust"

The paper closes with an explicit counterbalance to its own technology-forward argument, cautioning against uncritical over-reliance on tools: "There is a counterbalance for this lust for technology and people analytics... technology initiatives should be considered in view of the human factor that is the real driver of long-term success. People, not technology, are responsible for ensuring a steady progress toward the goal of attaining organisational excellence." The authors frame the "tipping point" as where futurism meets humanity, noting technology "can make or break the talent connection."

Dan Shapero, LinkedIn VP of Talent Solutions and Insights, quoted in Walford-Wright & Scott-Jackson (2018): "recruiting has always been an art, but it's becoming a science."
Dr. Brian Welle, Google Director of People Analytics, quoted in Walford-Wright & Scott-Jackson (2018): "if we have an employee base that reflects our [diverse] user base, we are going to better understand the needs of people all over the world."
Walford-Wright & Scott-Jackson (2018): "There is a counterbalance for this lust for technology and people analytics... People, not technology, are responsible for ensuring a steady progress toward the goal of attaining organisational excellence."

Practical implications for TS-ADR

Talent Rising operationalises "interpreting talent dashboards" at the acquisition stage: its four-tool selection stack shows concretely how raw candidate data becomes decision-ready insight (sifting → screening → selecting). At the same time, its own claim that automated, unbiased selection "removes the overriding human bias inherent in manual selection" is a useful discussion prompt for the course's AI bias risk ILO — algorithmic screening tools built on culture-fit and personality data can just as easily encode and scale bias as remove it if the underlying training data or fit criteria are not actively audited, a tension the paper's closing "technology lust" warning gestures toward without fully resolving.

Case Session 4

Bitstream

Harvard Business School Case 393-055 · Michael J. Roberts · 1992 (rev. 1998)

Jim Sole, the newly installed president of the $30-million Cambridge, MA font-technology company Bitstream, must decide how to fill a critical new role — general manager of a network printer-management product he believes could grow into a $100-million business — from a short list of four outside candidates surfaced by an executive recruiter. The case turns on how to weigh raw résumé pedigree, reference-check color, and cultural fit against Sole's own idiosyncratic hiring philosophy, which privileges attitude and aptitude over specific industry experience.

Company background, the network-printer opportunity, Jim Sole's hiring philosophy, the search process with headhunter Peter Dromeshauser, and the four resume-stage candidates (Cowan, Wendell, Fallon, Madison) with reference-check remarks.

Company background

Bitstream was founded in 1981 by four typographers from Linotype, a traditional typesetting firm, around the idea of digitized type fonts for display screens and printers. The core technical breakthrough was storing only one digitized bitmap per character per style and mathematically varying its size, rather than storing a separate bitmap for every size — a harder problem than it sounds, since a letter's height and stroke thickness do not shrink or grow at the same rate. By 1992 the company had $30 million in revenue split between two lines: $18 million in retail sales of shrink-wrapped font-library software through retail and direct corporate channels, and $12 million in OEM licensing revenue from hardware and software producers — Laserjet printers shipped with resident Bitstream fonts, and companies including Lotus and Microsoft licensed the technology for multi-font software capability. The firm had grown at roughly 25% annually in both revenue and headcount, reaching 200 employees by summer 1992 with 15% further growth forecast.

Jim Sole and his management approach

Sole was hired as president in January 1992, replacing a founder who had led the company since 1985 but whom the board (including the firm's investors) felt was not maximizing its potential; the chairman had served as acting president during the interim search. Sole had spent roughly twenty years in sales-management roles at IBM and Xerox, including growing a product-development unit at Xerox from $25 million to $250 million (1978–1983), before becoming president of a small electronic-publishing equipment company that grew from $8 million to $30 million and went public — until its founder, restored to control after the VCs cashed out post-IPO, fired him. On arriving at Bitstream, Sole moved to cut expenses roughly 15% below existing levels, replaced an individual bonus plan with a team bonus tied to a 5%/10% PBT target, banned personal attacks among staff, and instituted an MBO-like quarterly self-scoring process among peers. He described his general hiring philosophy:

Jim Sole: "The key is to get the right brain in the right job... I am generally not too worried about specific prior experience; I figure a smart person can learn what they need to know... Basic aptitude and attitude are much more important predictors for success."

The Network Print Manager opportunity

Bitstream identified an opening to build a network print-management product for Novell-based PC networks, addressing problems such as users not knowing whether a shared printer was jammed, queued, or loaded with the wrong font version. Sole sized the market by asking how large the company would be if it captured just 5% of Novell's installed base — the answer was $100 million, against Bitstream's existing $30 million base. Competitors identified in the space included Intel and Hewlett-Packard. Bitstream planned to sell through Novell's existing channel of VARs (value-added resellers, typically $10–20-million businesses earning two-thirds of revenue from network design, installation and support) rather than build a new channel from scratch. The envisioned unit was to grow into "a 50-person, $30-million business in 3-4 years."

The hiring dilemma and role design

Although Sole generally preferred promoting from within — arguing that outside candidates "look much better than internal ones" because they can more easily hide blemishes — he judged that VAR-channel and networking-product experience, which did not exist inside Bitstream, made an external hire necessary. He engaged executive recruiter Peter Dromeshauser, a twenty-plus-year veteran of technology-sector search whom he had worked with for two decades. Compensation was capped at $120,000 ($80,000 salary, $40,000 bonus potential, plus stock options), a level Sole felt was fair without disrupting the company's internal salary structure. Sole had not resolved the role's title or reporting line — torn between naming the hire VP of Networking Products reporting directly to him, versus a lower-profile arrangement to avoid friction from parachuting an outsider into a senior slot.

The search process

Dromeshauser described his method: after Sole named Novell, Banyan, Adobe, HP, Xerox, and Intel as target companies, he identified senior marketing executives with both product and channel knowledge at each, cold-called them without naming the client until interest was confirmed, and then interviewed in person. Of seven initially interested candidates, three were screened out for poor experience fit or personal chemistry, leaving four finalists. Dromeshauser offered a broader observation on hiring for growth companies:

Peter Dromeshauser: "Investors think that the only person who will be successful in the job is someone who has been successful in that role already... You can get a feel for someone's knowledge and experience through the resume and the interview, but it's much harder to judge the chemistry... people tend to hire in their own image."

The decision facing Jim Sole

With the four resumes and terse reference comments in hand, Sole is left to develop his own evaluation criteria — balancing his stated preference for aptitude and attitude over pedigree against a role he has acknowledged genuinely requires networking/VAR-specific experience — and to decide how to gather the further information needed for "a fair and accurate comparison" among candidates whose paper credentials look strong but whose personal fit with Bitstream's small, blunt, team-oriented culture remains largely untested.

Recruitment of a Star

Harvard Business School Case 9-407-036 · Boris Groysberg, Stephen Balog & Jennifer Haimson · 2006 (rev. 2007)

When Peter Thompson, Rubin, Stern & Hertz's star semiconductor equity analyst, resigns for a competitor just as RSH faces a major deal with client PowerChip, research director Stephen Connor must engineer a replacement — promoting Peter's junior analyst as a stopgap while running a full external search through headhunter Craig Robertson. The case is a granular study of star-hiring economics on Wall Street: the II (Institutional Investor) ranking system that manufactures "stars," the portability (or non-portability) of star performance across firms, and the tradeoffs among an established star, a regional up-and-comer, and promoting from within.

RSH's team-based research culture, the star departure and stopgap promotion of Rina Shea, the headhunter search and four finalist profiles (David Hughes, Gerald Baum, Sonia Meetha, Seth Horkum), interview findings, reference checks, and the open hiring decision.

RSH's research culture and the II ranking system

RSH's research department was built around teamwork rather than internal competition: senior analysts mentored juniors, bonuses were tied partly to group as well as individual success, and turnover was so low that departures "stung." Since 1972, Institutional Investor (II) magazine has published an annual "All-America Research Team" ranking analysts by sector at four tiers (first, second, third, runner-up) based on institutional client votes across six criteria: earnings forecasts, industry knowledge, overall service, accessibility/responsiveness, stock selection, and written reports. Ranked analysts become "stars," which the case explicitly ties to compensation stratification — quoting a research director noting the system is "by no means perfect" but "added transparency and efficiency," and recruiter Richard Lipstein observing that "the rise of II made stars of people quickly and threw compensation out of whack. These stars also became targets for other firms to try to hire."

The departure and stopgap promotion

Peter Thompson, RSH's only semiconductor analyst and a top performer, resigned abruptly for a competitor. Stephen Connor considered countering but decided against it, and instead split Peter's team by promoting his junior, Rina Shea, to senior analyst on a temporary basis to maintain PowerChip coverage while a permanent search proceeded. Peter was described by Connor as "incredibly intelligent," a driven "go-getter," but increasingly money-motivated and "extremely competitive" in ways that alienated colleagues in RSH's cooperative culture — establishing the profile against which replacement candidates would implicitly be measured.

Engaging the headhunter

Connor retained Craig Robertson of Superior Staffing Services ("Triple S"), a firm he had worked with for seven years, rather than search himself — reasoning that discretion mattered (news that RSH had lost its top semiconductor analyst could trigger competitors poaching further from the department) and that a search took more time than he had. Robertson surveyed 12 semiconductor analysts market-wide and reported that "practically no one wants to change firms right now," ultimately producing two II-ranked "star" candidates and two second-tier candidates, plus a fifth candidate who surfaced independently through a rival headhunter.

David Hughes (Spenser's and Company)

A well-seasoned analyst ranked in II for 15 consecutive years, ranked 2nd overall in the industry's October 2003 rankings (score 14.03) and currently a "second-teamer." Reference checks (from junior analyst Beth Tuck, previously at Spenser's) described him as "incredibly bright and articulate," a "fantastic mentor" enthusiastic about developing junior analysts, though sometimes imprecise about his own work (his research associate proofread for him) and "old-fashioned," resistant to the firm's newer technology. In person he impressed Connor with encyclopedic industry knowledge and Wall Street name recognition but raised age/stamina concerns — the case notes the job "tends to burn out analysts by 45 or 50." He wanted a multi-year compensation guarantee, which Connor found risky given the semiconductor industry's cyclicality (roughly two years of growth up to 20%, one slow year, one flat/declining year).

Gerald Baum (Gotz and Loeb)

Ranked 1st in electronics and 2nd in electrical-connectors industries; after covering semiconductors for only 18 months, already ranked 11th of over 100 analysts (score 2.03 in the October 2003 table), suggesting rapid rising talent. A reference check via colleague Jon Davidson (a former Gotz and Loeb employee) described the firm as hyper-competitive and not genuinely committed to analyst development. Baum admitted, unprompted, to having fired a junior analyst mid-client-presentation ("Casualties of my great work, I say!"), and had job-hopped three firms in six years. Two of Gotz and Loeb's top clients confirmed he "treated his staff terribly" but said they would follow him to a new firm regardless, given his stock-picking and client-service skills.

Sonia Meetha (Welsh, Harrison, and Smith)

Working at a small Stamford, CT boutique with minimal support staff (three analysts sharing two juniors), yet recognized by II as an "up-and-comer" — ranked 8th overall (score 6.56). A buy-side reference described her research as reliable, though not eager to travel (she has two young children), a fact some older RSH analysts explicitly worried about ("does she realize the position is a 24-hour/7-day-a-week job that requires a great deal of traveling?"). In interview she asked unusually detailed questions about RSH's culture, flexibility, mentoring structure, and how female analysts performed at the firm — asking to meet senior female analysts before asking a single question about compensation.

Seth Horkum (Jefferson Brothers)

Surfaced not through Robertson's search but via a cold call from a second headhunter, Anita Armstrong of Clifford Associates. Just ranked II runner-up (score 7.24, ranked 5th) after 15 years at Jefferson Brothers, with strong personal contacts at PowerChip. Brought a self-assembled packet of client testimonials to the interview. Described his motivation as frustration that Jefferson management would not recognize him as a "rising star," inadequate support staff, and insufficient compensation; expressed strong stated loyalty ("I've been with Jefferson Brothers for 15 years... I strongly believe in loyalty") despite actively job-hunting immediately after his ranking.

Second-round interviews and the decision point

All four candidates underwent two days of firm-wide interviews, generating a mix of enthusiastic and skeptical feedback — Baum's intelligence versus his "cocky" manner and refusal to bring his (unwilling) junior and assistant; Horkum's contagious enthusiasm versus suspicion about why he was job-hunting after 15 years; Meetha's poise versus doubts about her fit at a large firm and her family-driven travel limits; Hughes's legendary reputation versus explicit, unresolved concerns about his age and stamina. The case notes that star analyst compensation at the time ranged from $700,000 to a couple of million dollars, and that RSH's standard senior-analyst equity option package started at $100,000, growing to $300,000 after four years. With interviews complete and pressure mounting from his boss and the heads of sales and trading to "hire someone as expeditiously as you can," Stephen Connor is left to weigh cost, fit, and the portability of each candidate's "star" performance to a new platform — including whether Rina Shea, the internal stopgap, might in fact be the right permanent choice.

Practical implications

The case directly interrogates whether star performance is portable across firms — a central question for any strategic-recruitment framework that leans on poaching proven talent rather than developing it internally — and illustrates how reference checks, informal networks, and headhunter intermediation function as screening tools that go well beyond the resume, alongside the fairness question raised by colleagues' explicit doubts about a candidate's suitability tied to her status as a mother of young children.

Article Session 4

The Legal and Ethical Implications of Using AI in Hiring

Harvard Business Review (HBR.org) · Ben Dattner, Tomas Chamorro-Premuzic, Richard Buchband & Lucinda Schettler · April 25, 2019

Four ManpowerGroup-affiliated authors survey how AI-driven hiring tools — game-based assessments, social-media scraping bots, linguistic analysis, and video interviews that read tone, emotion, and nonverbal behavior — are outpacing the legal and ethical frameworks meant to govern candidate assessment. Unlike longstanding validated psychometrics, these new tools risk inferring protected or private traits (disability, sexual orientation, mental health) without consent, exposing employers to disparate-impact and privacy liability even when no discriminatory intent exists.

Historical baseline of validated psychometrics, the legal frameworks at risk (ADA, EEOC adverse-impact doctrine, Employee Polygraph Protection Act, Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act, GDPR vs. U.S. patchwork privacy law), the three named privacy dilemmas, the Amazon resume-screening example, and the authors' concluding call for public debate.

The new toolkit versus the old

The article opens by cataloguing the new generation of AI hiring tools: game-based assessments, bots scraping social media postings, linguistic analysis of candidates' writing, and video-based interviews using algorithms to analyze speech content, tone of voice, emotional states, nonverbal behavior, and "temperamental clues." These are contrasted with longstanding, scientifically validated psychometric instruments — the authors name the NEO-PI-R, the Wonderlic Test, Raven's Progressive Matrices, and the Hogan Personality Inventory — which have decades of peer-reviewed validation against job performance. Psychometric testing traces back over 100 years to the U.S. military's Army Alpha test, which sorted recruits by category and likely role success; psychometrics traditionally split into cognitive/intelligence, personality/temperament, and mental-health/clinical categories.

Legal foundations at risk

Since the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990, employers are generally barred from inquiring about or using physical disability, mental health, or clinical diagnosis in pre-employment assessment. The article notes the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) issued guidance roughly a decade prior to publication that expanding psychiatric-literature definitions of personality disorders could qualify as protected mental impairments, and that the ADA Amendments Act broadened who counts as disabled — potentially covering people with social-communication difficulties, concentration issues, or interpersonal difficulty. Cognitive-ability testing is flagged as a reliable predictor of job success but legally vulnerable to disparate-impact claims by gender, race, age, or national origin, requiring employers to prove job-relatedness if adverse impact is shown; personality assessments are described as carrying less discrimination risk because they correlate weakly with protected demographic variables. The authors also cite the Employee Polygraph Protection Act (barring lie-detector tests as pre-employment screens) and the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (barring use of genetic information in employment decisions) as frameworks that new AI tools could functionally circumvent by inferring the same underlying information through other means, and note Senate concern specifically about facial-analysis technologies and equal opportunity.

Three privacy dilemmas the authors pose

The article structures its ethical analysis around three explicit questions. First, on personal attributes: AI and big data increasingly infer "proxy" variables for protected traits — the authors note Facebook "likes" can already be used to infer sexual orientation and race "with considerable accuracy," and predict that face-reading AI may soon discern sexual orientation, political orientation, and mood/emotion with high accuracy, raising unresolved questions about how the ADA should apply. Second, on lifestyle and activities: big data can surface a candidate's church attendance, a review of a relative's dementia-care facility, or a divorce filing — the authors cite the statistic that 90% of the world's data was generated in just the two years preceding publication. They contrast the EU's harmonized General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) with the U.S.'s "patchwork" state-by-state privacy approach, noting more than twenty states have passed laws (since 2012) barring employers from demanding social-media passwords, alongside California legislation constraining employer use of candidate/employee data. Third, on disabilities, the article returns to the ADA's post-Amendments-Act expansion and flags algorithmic risk of inadvertently screening on protected impairments.

The Amazon example and bias mitigation

The article's central named real-world controversy is Amazon, which "developed an automated talent search program to review resumes — which was abandoned once the company realized that the program was not rating candidates in a gender-neutral way." The authors frame balanced training data as the primary technical mitigation: "To reduce such biases, developers are balancing the data used for training AI models, to appropriately represent all groups."

Dattner, Chamorro-Premuzic, Buchband & Schettler: "Employers recognize that they can't or shouldn't ask candidates about their family status or political orientation, or whether they are pregnant, straight, gay, sad, lonely, depressed, physically or mentally ill, drinking too much, abusing drugs, or sleeping too little. However, new technologies may already be able to discern many of these factors indirectly and without proper (or even any) consent."

Conclusion

The authors close without prescriptive answers, framing the piece as a call for public discussion: "There are no easy answers to many of the new questions about privacy we have raised here, but we believe that they are all worthy of public discussion and debate."

Practical implications

The article is the course's clearest anchor for the "fair, inclusive and unbiased talent practices" objective: it demonstrates that screening-tool effectiveness cannot be assessed on accuracy or cost alone — a tool can be predictive and still be illegal or unethical if it relies on unvalidated proxy inferences about protected or private traits, as the Amazon case shows even sophisticated employers can build biased screening tools inadvertently.

Article Session 4

Making the Most of On-Line Recruiting

Harvard Business Review (Tool Kit, Reprint r0103k) · Peter Cappelli · March 2001

Wharton's Peter Cappelli argues that the internet has turned recruiting into a true, wide-open labor market that companies must treat like a marketing function — with a four-step process of attracting, sorting, contacting, and finally closing candidates by human touch. Drawing on a year of research into online recruiting vendors and practices, he documents dramatic cost and speed advantages alongside serious, underappreciated legal risk in automated screening and a genuine retention threat from the same tools that make hiring easier.

The scale and economics of online recruiting, the "recruiting as marketing" reframing, the four-step hiring process (attracting, sorting, contacting, closing) with named vendors and companies, the discrimination sidebar, and the retention/defense implications.

Scale and cost economics

Cappelli opens with consultant Ed Melia's demonstration that a simple search engine query can surface 567 pages of résumés for currently-employed IBM software engineers who are not job-hunting — "passive" candidates locatable through chat rooms, Usenet groups, and by "flipping the URL" to find corporate intranets. He reports that hiring online costs roughly one-twentieth as much as hiring through want ads and traditional means. A Recruitsoft/iLogos Research study of 50 Fortune 500 companies found the average company cut about six days off a 43-day hiring cycle by posting jobs online instead of in newspapers, another four days by taking online applications instead of paper ones, and over a week by screening and processing applications electronically. He notes 90% of large U.S. companies were already recruiting via the internet, with Monster.com drawing about 4 million job searchers on a typical Monday and holding a database of more than 18 million employee profiles/résumés — representing 13% of the U.S. labor force.

Recruiting as marketing

Cappelli's central reframing is that recruiting must be run like marketing: candidates should be "carefully identified and targeted, attracted to the company and its brand, and then sold on the job." He cites a WetFeet.com survey finding 20% of job seekers had applied to a company as a result of seeing a product ad, and gives named examples of employer branding: GE Power Systems's homepage links on diversity and work-life balance; Booz-Allen & Hamilton showcasing a pro bono Special Olympics engagement; Accenture and Enterprise Rent-a-Car providing "compelling materials" on why to work there. On referral-based recruiting marketing, he cites SelectMinds (alumni-network platforms — 8,000 former employees joined New York Life Insurance's alumni network in its first month), Referrals.com (paid confidential leads), and Monster.com's My Monster subscription service. Cisco Systems is cited for using technical online libraries to identify high-potential engineer-candidates through usage tracking, and Fidelity Investments for mining applicant databases for marketing research.

Step 1 — Attracting; Step 2 — Sorting

Sorting is described as increasingly automated: basic questions ("Are you willing to move?") screen obvious mismatches, and vendors like Brainbench (skills certification), CIC Applicant Background Checks's HireCheck (background verification), and Kforce.com (audio/video applicant clips) extend this. A Recruitsoft/iLogos survey found 12% of employers tested applicants online. Named company screening examples: PricewaterhouseCoopers used sophisticated online psychometric instruments; J.P. Morgan Chase built a game-based online application for college students; Allstate Insurance ran a stepwise screening program scoring independent-agent applicants against a "model successful agent" profile with immediate score feedback at each stage.

Step 3 — Making contact quickly; hiring management systems

Speed is framed as decisive — PNC Bank's online recruiting specialist Shannon Wong is quoted: "Now we have to get to good candidates within 24 hours, or we lose them." Hiring management systems (HMS) from vendors like BrassRing Systems, Icarian, and Recruitsoft automate collection, screening, source-tracking, and time-to-fill calculation; Cappelli notes only about 10% of large companies used latest-generation HMS at the time. Named organizational examples: Union Pacific's HMS lets applicants check application status; Humana's system auto-prompts applicants to update résumés every six months; IBM and Kellogg outsourced recruitment to Manpower and Bernard Hodes Group respectively; Boston Scientific used RecruitUSA to target job-board postings. Some firms moved recruiting organizationally out of HR entirely — cited example: GlobalNetwork Companies moved recruiting to report to business development.

Step 4 — Closing the deal

Cappelli frames closing as the one phase where the internet should not be overused: "good applicants have lots of opportunities and need to be sold on why one job is superior to others." Ed Melia is cited recommending recruiters spend only about an hour a day working online, reserving the rest of their time for relationship-building and closing; some companies send recruiters to sales training for this purpose, or split "finding" and "closing" into separate roles, or use intermediary-matching sites like BrilliantPeople.com.

Discrimination: a serious legal risk (sidebar)

Cappelli's sidebar is the article's sharpest fairness warning: hiring criteria that reject disproportionate numbers of women, minorities, people with disabilities, or workers over 40 are illegal under U.S. law unless shown to predict job performance, and courts have held employers liable for their outsourced vendors' discriminatory screening techniques even when the vendor validated its own tests independently. He notes online credit checks are illegal under most state law unless credit is shown necessary to the job, and that personality tests probing psychological problems can be treated by courts as medical exams prohibited under the ADA for applicants. He quotes R. Wendell Williams, founder of ScientificSelection.com: "Seldom do they want to listen to what I have to say" regarding valid selection-test design, and Vince Webb of MRI Worldwide observing companies "are a bit more cavalier on-line with respect to recruiting." He cites Cigna avoiding online screening/testing over legal concerns, and Texas Instruments's workaround of giving applicants a self-scored personality/fit test without recording the data, which most low scorers self-select out from. He also flags that famous "objective" proxies like Jeff Bezos at Amazon.com reportedly asking applicants their SAT scores must still be validated against actual job performance to avoid adverse-impact liability, and that EU data-protection directives restrict cross-border movement of applicant data collected online.

The retention threat ("A Solid Defense")

Cappelli warns online recruiting is "a two-edged sword": if it's easier for a company to hire experienced workers, it's equally easy for competitors to poach its own staff, and proliferating online salary data (e.g., Robert Half International salary surveys posted on Monster.com) intensifies pay comparison and turnover pressure. He cites Stanford psychologist Charles O'Reilly's research finding more job choices reduce employee commitment. Defensive tactics named include Cigna changing IT employees' email addresses to frustrate recruiters and Hewitt Associates monitoring whether its own employees have résumés posted on job boards; a more constructive approach cited is Nortel Networks's internal job board "Job Shop," built with Monster.com, which let any employee post a résumé confidentially and any manager post an opening.

The future of online recruiting

Cappelli closes by describing early standardization efforts — the HR-XML Consortium, whose members (including Aetna, IBM, Towers Perrin, Watson Wyatt Worldwide, Monster.com, Hire.com, and CareerBuilder) worked toward common job/applicant-description standards — and rising use of third-party skill certification, citing U.S. Department of Education analyst Clifford Adelman's estimate of over 5,000 independent skill-assessment sites administering roughly 3 million skill assessments in 1999, with roughly one in eight IT jobs already requiring a third-party certification. He notes executive recruiting was also moving online through Korn/Ferry International's Futurestep and Heidrick & Struggles International's LeadersOnline.

Practical implications

Cappelli's four-step framework (attract/sort/contact/close) is a direct, still-relevant map for evaluating recruitment-source strategy and screening-tool effectiveness — and his discrimination sidebar anticipates the AI-hiring article's legal concerns nearly two decades earlier, making the pairing a natural before/after comparison for the course's fairness objective.

Article Session 4

LinkedIn as a New Selection Method: Psychometric Properties and Assessment Approach

Personnel Psychology, 72, 187–211 · Nicolas Roulin & Julia Levashina · 2019

Roulin and Levashina run the first systematic psychometric evaluation of LinkedIn as a selection tool, testing whether rater assessments of applicant profiles show acceptable interrater reliability, temporal stability, convergent validity against self-reports, predictive validity against career outcomes, and adverse impact. Across two studies with real job-seeking business students and MBA raters, they find LinkedIn outperforms prior Facebook-based research on skills and hiring-recommendation reliability, but that validity is real only for visible traits (Extraversion, leadership, planning, communication, cognitive ability) — and that itemized, structured rating scales substantially outperform holistic "gut feel" assessments.

The realistic accuracy model (RAM) framework, Study 1 method (133 applicants, two MBA rater cohorts, six hypotheses) and its reliability/validity/adverse-impact/profile-feature results, Study 2's global-vs-itemized comparison, and the authors' practical recommendations.

Motivation and theoretical framework

The authors note that although surveys suggest over 40% of job seekers use LinkedIn to find jobs, 94% of hiring managers use it for recruitment, and 85% use it for selection purposes, almost no empirical research had tested whether LinkedIn-based judgments meet the field's established selection-method criteria: reliability, validity, legality (no adverse impact), and standardization. They build on Funder's realistic accuracy model (RAM), which holds that judgment accuracy depends on four conditions — the trait must be behaviorally relevant to the information environment, that information must be available to the rater, the rater must detect it, and the rater must correctly utilize it. RAM predicts that more visible traits (backed by more available information) will be assessed more accurately than less visible ones, and the authors argue LinkedIn — unlike Facebook — offers rich information about skills and job history but comparatively little about personality or private life, since LinkedIn profiles are more static and work-focused. A preliminary survey of 70 North American hiring managers found they rated LinkedIn roughly equivalent to résumés for personality and predictive validity, slightly less valid than résumés for skills/cognitive ability, and less valid than interviews across the board.

Study 1 — sample and design

The sample comprised 133 senior business students (60% Canadian, 40% U.S.), actively job- or internship-seeking, mean age 21.48 (SD = 2.67), gender-balanced (49% female), 64.5% White, 22.3% Asian, 5% Black, 2.5% Middle Eastern, 1.7% Hispanic. Participants completed the Mini-IPIP (20-item Big Five personality measure), self-report skill scales (adapted from Woo et al., 2008, covering leadership, planning, communication, teamwork, information seeking, problem solving, conflict management, adaptability), and the Wonderlic Personnel Test (WPT-Q), an 8-minute, 30-item cognitive-ability test. Their real, publicly visible LinkedIn profiles were rated twice, a year apart, by two independent groups of MBA raters (nine raters at Time 1, eight at Time 2, each profile scored by two raters) using five-point Likert scales for skills/personality, a one-item IQ-estimate method for cognitive ability, and a five-item hiring-recommendation scale (α = 0.95 at T1, 0.96 at T2). Objective profile features — word count, presence of a picture, number of connections, number of skill endorsements, number of groups, description depth — were separately coded. Career success (job aligned with degree, management role, promotion, number of relevant jobs) was coded roughly two years after initial ratings.

Study 1 — interrater reliability and temporal stability

Interrater reliability (ICC) averaged 0.45 (T1) and 0.40 (T2) for skills, 0.37 (T1) and 0.54 (T2) for personality, 0.63 (T1) and 0.57 (T2) for cognitive ability, and 0.58 (T1) rising to 0.75 (T2) for hiring recommendations — generally supporting Hypothesis 1, though conflict management (ICC = 0.03 at T2) and leadership (0.04 at T2) were notably unreliable. One-year temporal stability correlations ranged from r = 0.39 (conflict management) to r = 0.65 (Extraversion), averaging around r = 0.51–0.52 across skills and personality, with cognitive ability at r = 0.58 and hiring recommendations at r = 0.52 — all statistically significant (p < .01), supporting Hypothesis 2. Controlling for profile updates (participants added an average of 98.07 words, SD = 174.36, between the two ratings) slightly increased stability for skills, cognitive ability, and recommendations, but not for personality.

Study 1 — convergent and predictive validity

Convergent validity (correlation with self-reports) was significant only for the more "visible" traits, consistent with RAM: leadership (average r = 0.26, p < .01), planning (r = 0.23, p < .05), communication (r = 0.22, p < .05), and Extraversion (r = 0.20, p < .05) — but not for less-visible skills like information seeking, problem solving, or conflict management (rs of 0.04–0.14, all non-significant), nor for the other four personality traits (rs from −0.02 to 0.10). Teamwork showed lower-than-expected validity (r = 0.11, ns). Cognitive-ability ratings correlated with actual Wonderlic scores at average r = 0.30 (p < .01), driven mainly by a stronger T2 correlation of r = 0.38. On predictive validity, LinkedIn-based hiring recommendations were significantly associated with obtaining a degree-aligned job (average r = 0.20, p < .05), number of degree-aligned jobs held (r = 0.25, p < .01), and having been promoted (r = 0.20, p < .05) — but not with holding a management role (r = 0.09, ns).

Study 1 — adverse impact and profile features

No significant gender difference was found in hiring recommendations (d = 0.07, p = .68). A small-to-moderate difference favored White over non-White profiles (M = 3.69 vs. 3.38, d = 0.34, p = .06) and Canadian over U.S. profiles (d = 0.34, p = .05), though the ethnicity/country effects disappeared once other profile features were statistically controlled — the authors note White students were more likely to have a profile picture (96% vs. 80%) and Canadians averaged more connections (169 vs. 128), suggesting the raw group difference reflected profile content differences rather than direct bias. Regression analysis found profile length (word count) explained 37% of variance in hiring recommendations on its own (B = 0.62, p < .01); adding picture presence, connections, and endorsements explained a further 21%. Having a profile picture (B = 0.26, p < .01) and more connections (B = 0.26, p < .01) both independently predicted higher ratings, but skill endorsements, a "professional" (vs. casual) photo, number of skills listed, and group memberships did not. Some of these cues were shown to be partially valid signals: longer profiles correlated with self-reported Conscientiousness (r = 0.22); having a picture correlated with cognitive ability (r = 0.40), Extraversion (r = 0.18), and communication (r = 0.21); more connections correlated with Extraversion (r = 0.24), leadership (r = 0.26), planning (r = 0.25), and communication (r = 0.22).

Study 2 — global versus itemized assessment

A second sample of 24 U.S. MBA students (46% female, 79% White) each rated 20 of the Study 1 profiles — ten using a holistic global hiring-recommendation-only judgment, ten using an itemized approach scoring each of the eight skills, Big Five traits, and cognitive ability individually before a final recommendation. Interrater reliability for the unstructured global rating was low (ICC = 0.38); itemized ratings were markedly more reliable — skills ICCs 0.43–0.60, personality ICCs 0.49–0.62 (except Emotional Stability at 0.24), cognitive ability at 0.47, and the itemized hiring recommendation at ICC = 0.60, nearly double the global approach. Itemizing also changed adverse-impact patterns: global ratings showed no gender gap (d = 0.04) but a significant White-favoring ethnicity gap (d = 0.50, p < .01); itemized ratings eliminated the ethnicity gap (d = 0.31, ns) but introduced a gap favoring women (d = 0.57, p < .01) — only partial support for the hypothesis that structuring reduces bias.

Overall psychometric summary and conclusion

Summarizing across both studies, the authors report LinkedIn's interrater reliability for skills and hiring recommendations exceeds that previously found for Facebook-based KSAO ratings (ICC = 0.14) and suitability ratings (ICC = 0.23) in Van Iddekinge et al. (2016), consistent with RAM's prediction that LinkedIn makes job-relevant information more available even though it makes personality-relevant information less available than Facebook. They frame their convergent-validity correlations (roughly r = 0.20–0.30) as "moderate" by applied-psychology benchmarks, and conclude that LinkedIn "represents a superior alternative to Facebook" as a cybervetting tool, while cautioning that its value is concentrated in a narrow set of visible, job-relevant constructs.

Roulin & Levashina: "Hiring managers can reliably assess and make valid inferences about Extraversion, planning or communication skills, and cognitive abilities using LinkedIn. However, they should refrain from attempting to assess conflict management, adaptability, or personality traits other than Extraversion."

Practical implications

This is the course's most direct empirical evidence on screening-tool effectiveness: it shows a widely used, low-cost screening practice (cybervetting via LinkedIn) has real but narrow validity, that unstructured "eyeball" judgments are markedly less reliable and more bias-prone than itemized scoring rubrics, and that superficial profile features (a photo, connection count) drive hiring recommendations even where they are not always valid signals of underlying qualifications — a direct empirical warning for any organization treating LinkedIn profile polish as a proxy for candidate quality.

Case Session 5

Building a Training Culture at Montecarlo Limited

Ivey Publishing · Jatin Christie and Jignesh Shah · 2024

In January 2021, Jignesh Shah, senior vice-president of HR at Montecarlo Limited (MCL), a 25-year-old Indian infrastructure and construction company, is weighing whether a heavy-industry firm whose culture prizes outcomes and deliverables should invest in building a genuine "training culture" rather than the perfunctory, calendar-driven training typical of the sector. Having already automated MCL's HR processes, Shah must decide how to move the L&D function from a compliance exercise into a true engine of employee growth, self-discovery, and organizational excellence.

Full case digest: MCL's history and culture, the automation and competency infrastructure, the four training types and ADDIE-based delivery model, FY2022–2023 programming, and Shah's open decision on whether a training culture can take root

Company background

Montecarlo Limited (MCL) was established in 1995 by Kanubhai Patel, the son of a farmer from Isanpur village in North Gujarat, who came to Ahmedabad to build an independent business after nearly 20 years working jointly with his three brothers. Patel is described in the case as an embodiment of "rags to riches" success achieved through humility, character, and a servant-leadership style; the case authors explicitly liken him to a "level-five leader" as defined by Jim Collins in Good to Great. MCL began in water and irrigation works tied to the Narmada River, expanded into road works in Madhya Pradesh from 2001, and from 2005 diversified into highways, mining, power, railways (2014), energy infrastructure (2011), and building and factory construction. By January 2021 MCL had grown from a small local outfit into a nationally recognized infrastructure company with revenue that had crossed ₹36,000 million (with US$1 = ₹72.9093 in January 2021). It held ISO 9001:2015 (quality management), ISO 14001:2015 (environmental management), and ISO 45001:2018 (occupational health and safety) certifications, and had run on the SAP platform since 2008 — an early and unusual step for a construction company. The joint managing directors, Brijesh Patel and Mrunal Patel, Kanubhai Patel's sons, jointly led the company and championed its cultural and technological transformation. MCL's chosen values were nurturance, commitment, integrity, ownership, and alignment, reinforced through practices such as an internal e-magazine, Montecarlo One (carrying human-interest stories, employee family achievements, quizzes, and creative writing), festival celebrations, health camps, vigilance programs, a cricket league and a chess league, yoga workshops, tree-planting drives, and "zero-harm" camps. The company's CSR arm, the Montecarlo Foundation, supported education, primary health care, and skill-building programs. Jignesh Shah, referred to in the case as "Dr. Shah," joined MCL as vice-president of HR in July 2017 after three decades of HR experience across engineering, pharmaceutical, petrochemical, polyester, FMCG, power, and infrastructure companies.

HR automation as the platform for L&D transformation

MCL was one of the few construction companies to have automated its entire HR function — recruitment and selection, induction, training, and performance management — accessible via mobile phone and built on SAP SuccessFactors alongside dedicated environmental health and safety software. Automation covered budgeting, competency-based recruiting, payroll, training needs identification (TNI) and delivery, transfers, resignations, travel, and card issuance, and initially met employee resistance rooted in unfamiliarity before proving its worth. Shah viewed this automation as the springboard for integrating the L&D and HR functions and for moving MCL beyond MCL's industry norm of routine, technical, mandatory training toward a genuine "culture of training" — one meant to help employees engage in self-discovery, close knowledge-skills gaps, and build a broader professional repertoire rather than simply tick a compliance box.

The training-culture problem MCL faced

The case frames the central tension explicitly: training in the construction and infrastructure industry was traditionally technical, needs-driven, mandatory, or routine, following a repeating annual calendar that let companies claim they trained employees without offering real opportunities for reflection, development, and growth. Shah's ambition — with the backing of the joint MDs — was to build not just a training infrastructure but a "positive and healthy mindset toward training" among MCL's workforce, making training central to employee experience, engagement, and development. This was explicitly new territory for a construction firm, and Shah anticipated a "bumpy" road.

Building the training infrastructure: SOPs, competencies, and ADDIE

The first step was developing standard operating procedures (SOPs) for every department, undertaken with consulting firm KPMG through an initiative called Project Udaan ("flight"), which underpinned the design of all subsequent training. MCL built a competency-based system using scientific tools to assess role requirements against employee ability and identify development gaps. It defined 46 pre-identified functional competencies (mapped against all 142 roles in the company) and 10 types of behavioural competencies, forming the basis of TNI and training assignment via a quarterly training calendar. MCL offered four broad training types: (1) onboarding induction training for new hires, (2) mandatory training on SOPs, (3) functional training based on competency mapping, and (4) behavioural training based on behavioural competencies. All training design followed the ADDIE model (analysis, design, development, implementation, evaluation). Functional, mandatory, and induction training was delivered by in-house trainers — department heads or nominees who supplemented pre-designed modules with their own experience — while behavioural training, typically a one-day program, was outsourced to external trainers.

Training needs identification, delivery mechanics, and evaluation

TNI was conducted by functional heads using interview evaluations, probation evaluations, internal audits, on-the-job observation, and the performance management system, with needs uploaded to SuccessFactor, which allotted trainers to trainees by date and calendar. Group sizes ran to about 20 people per session, with sessions lasting two to three hours and delivered mostly via standard PowerPoint lecture format with limited interactivity; trainee lists mixed seniority levels by department rather than grade. A given employee could, in principle, repeat a program within six months, though repetition was rare in practice, and content and trainer stayed constant across iterations even as the trainee cohort changed. Training effectiveness measurement was still "evolving": HODs and supervisors provided feedback roughly six months after training, but MCL lacked a more rigorous or immediate effectiveness metric.

FY 2022–2023 training activity

In FY2022–2023, MCL ran classroom and online sessions on communication, execution focus, and managing teams; delivered content through weekly "micro learning packs"; ran a behavioural interview workshop to professionalize HR recruiting; conducted vendor-partner technical workshops on bearings in bridges/viaducts and concrete design mix; and covered drawing awareness, environment/health/safety, AutoCAD, and quality management. Broader programming spanned leadership, negotiation, communication, team building, stress management, induction/orientation, and functional/technical topics.

The decision facing Jignesh Shah

As of the case date, Shah is uncertain whether the training culture MCL has begun building will take hold, whether the training process is genuinely effective, and what sustained effort will be needed for employees to internalize L&D as a core value rather than a mandated activity — believing the culture will only "come full circle" once employees take ownership of their own development. The open strategic question posed to the reader is whether MCL, operating in an outcomes-focused heavy industry, should continue investing in a people- and learning-centred culture at all, and if so, how to make its still-evolving training-evaluation mechanism robust enough to prove the investment's worth.

Practical implications

MCL's 46-competency framework mapped to 142 roles is a clear example of operationalizing the skills/competencies distinction taught in this session: functional training addresses discrete technical skills tied to a role, while behavioural training targets broader, transferable competencies (teamwork, communication, leadership) that cut across roles. The case's unresolved effectiveness-measurement gap — feedback collected only via HOD observation six months out — illustrates why building genuine organizational capability (not just delivering training hours) requires evaluation systems that go beyond attendance and satisfaction.

Case Session 5

Eyes of Janus: Evaluating Learning and Development at Tata Motors

Ivey Publishing (Richard Ivey School of Business Foundation) · Debolina Dutta and Professor Mathew J. Manimala · 2014

In January 2013, Tata Motors' chief human resources officer closes a performance review of chief talent officer Vikram Bector's L&D function by invoking the Roman god Janus — who looks simultaneously forward and back — to ask whether Tata Motors Academy (TMA), the company's ambitious corporate university, has been so focused on expanding its reach that it has neglected to rigorously evaluate its own past effectiveness and financial return. Bector must prepare to defend TMA's investment in an upcoming budget meeting even as he wrestles with immature metrics for linking training to business outcomes.

Full case digest: Tata Motors' turnaround context, the human capital strategy and ACES framework, Tata Motors Academy's structure and eight guiding principles, the Autonova tx and iteach programs, external recognition, and the unresolved evaluation challenge

Organizational context

Tata Motors Limited (TML), founded in 1945 as Tata Engineering and Locomotive Company (TELCO) in pre-Independence India, was the country's oldest and largest automobile company, with consolidated annual turnover of US$32.5 billion in 2011/12. Its first commercial vehicle launched in 1954 in collaboration with Daimler-Benz AG (terminated 1969), after which TML pursued collaborations extending into South Korea, Spain, Brazil, Thailand, South Africa, and the United Kingdom; it had been listed on the New York Stock Exchange since September 2004. By 2010 TML employed roughly 49,000 people, served customers through over 3,500 distributors and service centres, and ran R&D centres in India plus three abroad (South Korea, Spain, UK). TML suffered losses in financial year 2000/01 (attributed to the Asian financial crisis and rising domestic competition), recovered sharply from 2001/02, then entered a period of renewed stagnation from 2006/07. Its response was a strategy of disruptive innovation, producing the Tata Indica (initiated 1998, launched 2004) and the Tata Ace (launched May 2005), India's first mini truck, engineered to a strict sub-INR100,000 cost target while meeting safety and performance requirements. TML also introduced the "New Product Introduction" (NPI) group and a "things gone right/things gone wrong" (TGR/TGW) documentation template, disseminated through divisional meetings, project reviews, and the company intranet "MyTataMotors."

The human capital strategy and ACES

In 2010, TML's CHRO spearheaded a new human capital strategy aimed at making TML "a world-class destination for best-in-class talent," anchored in the company's vision of being "most admired by our customers, employees, business partners and shareholders" and structured around three planks: Talent and Leadership, Organizational Renewal, and Organizational Enablers. The strategy's cultural engine was ACES — Accountability, Customer and Product Focus, Excellence, and Speed — an internally conceived acronym meant to make new cultural priorities easy to understand and adopt.

Tata Motors Academy: structure and programs

Tata Motors Academy (TMA), a corporate university established at TML's Mumbai headquarters in early 2011, served employees plus dealers, vendors, and contract employees across four learning areas: functional and technical skills, management skills, mandatory programs (induction, compliance), and global leadership skills. It was organized into six centres of excellence (COEs) — Manufacturing Excellence and Innovation, Operator Training, Commercial Training, Dealer Training, Corporate Training, and Behavioural Training — supported by a Learning Support Services (LSS) department and drawing additionally on the Tata Group's Tata Management Training Centre, universities, business schools, and external consultants. Roughly 60 to 70 per cent of TMA's programs addressed functional and technical capability and were customized locally, while management and global programs were designed centrally; all behavioural training aligned to the Tata Leadership Practice (TLP), the common leadership and competency framework across Tata Group companies. The flagship six-month Executive Development Program (EDP) on Global Leadership, offered to newly promoted and appointed executive-grade employees, illustrates TMA's design evolution: originally a 14-day, nearly all-classroom residential program, it was redesigned in 2012 based on participant feedback into a blend of e-learning, classroom learning, and on-field internships, adding a four-to-six-day cross-domain orientation module in which participants worked alongside other departments. The redesign followed the "Six Disciplines of Breakthrough Learning" (6D) methodology — Define, Design, Deliver, Drive, Deploy, Document — popularized by the U.S.-based Fort Hill Company. CTO Vikram Bector captured the scale challenge:

Vikram Bector, CTO, Tata Motors: "With nearly 49,000 employees and 2,000 dealers having thousands of salesmen, creating an impact through training is like trying to boil the ocean! With the industry changing rapidly and higher levels of automation coming in, TMA needs to constantly redefine the content and reach to match the ever expanding profile of learners."

The eight guiding principles of TMA

TMA operated under eight explicit principles. (1) Alignment to Business Strategy, governed by a Learning Advisory Council (LAC) that shifted training ownership from HR/L&D alone to the business; its flagship example was Autonova tx (technical excellence), a Manufacturing Excellence COE program targeting the top five product-quality issues in commercial vehicles, which had trained over 3,297 employees by 2012 and was tracked via a CIRO (context, input, reaction, outcome) feedback framework measuring content, implementation, opportunity to apply, and trainer quality, alongside pre/post knowledge-gain testing. (2) Leadership Involvement, embodied in the 2011 "iteach" flagship program, which trained internal subject-matter experts as certified trainers (facilitation skills taught with an external partner); by November 2012 there were more than 300 certified iteach trainers, with a target of at least 25 per cent from senior executive grades (deputy general manager and above), each earning 200 reward points per training hour redeemable for non-monetary rewards like tablets and laptops — intended partly to capture tacit knowledge ahead of the retirement of over 3,500 mid-to-senior employees within five years. (3) Efficiency in Training Delivery, tracked via participants, programs delivered, utilization rates, and cost per participant, with a mixed centralized/decentralized budget model and early overseas e-learning connectivity via TMA-Online. (4) Training Effectiveness, assessed on five criteria — delivery quality, knowledge gain, application to job, business impact, and value — with a "business outcome rating" statistically linking participant opinion to predictive job-performance impact, aided by the Fort Hill Company's learning impact calculator. (5) Appropriate L&D Investment, with close to 50 per cent of training delivered internally and rising investment in certified trainers. (6) Broad-basing Learning Opportunities, including knowledge portals, e-learning, coaching/mentoring, and communities of practice — notably the Community of Project Management Practitioners (COPMP). The TMA-online platform, launched April 2012, hosted over 3,000 online courses, 18,000 e-books, and 750 videos, delivering roughly half of even formal program content, and was accessed by over 49,704 people across TML, its subsidiaries, and channel partners. (7) Competency of the L&D team itself, developed through Learning Management Solutions training, 6D process training, and iteach facilitation certification, though gaps between needed and available investment persisted. (8) Measurement of Training Outcomes, still under development at group and individual levels via Bector's internal dashboard metrics.

Recognition and internal validation

TML was named "Best Learning Organization-Asia (2011-12)" at a Mumbai ceremony on June 7, 2012, judged by a panel chaired by Dr. T.V. Rao ("the Father of Indian HRD") and including Robert Whitman (Global Chairman and CEO, Franklin Covey), Sarah Cook (author, Essential Guide to Employee Engagement), Kishore Dash (Thunderbird School of Global Management), Ganesh Natarajan (CEO, Zensar), and Adil Malia (Group President-HR, Essar Group). An email from the Assistant General Manager of the Auto Products Unit, Pune, praised the Autonova initiative's first anniversary:

Assistant General Manager, Auto Products Unit, Pune: "As we sit on the first anniversary of the Autonova initiative, I would be the first to admit to the reservations I had on all of this... I never believed that knowledge could be transferred in this way... Today, after a year, I salute the perseverance of the entire team. The juggernaut has started and I can see the change!"

An internal TMA survey found 74 per cent of respondents agreed or strongly agreed the programs increased their work competency, and 79 per cent agreed TMA gave them ample opportunity to learn and grow.

The evaluation question Bector faces

Despite this success, the CHRO's closing "Eyes of Janus" metaphor frames the open decision: should TMA continue looking only forward — expanding programs, scale, and reach — or must it now also look backward, rigorously evaluating past effectiveness and building credible financial-return metrics, before the forthcoming budget meeting where senior management will press for demonstrated ROI? Bector must also decide how to focus a geographically dispersed, matrix-structured team on a few high-impact activities rather than diluting its charter across too many initiatives.

Practical implications

TMA's structure operationalizes the skills/competencies/capabilities ladder directly: functional/technical COEs build discrete skills, the Tata Leadership Practice defines cross-cutting behavioural competencies, and the mass-scale "capability and competency building" Bector describes aims at organization-wide capability. The iteach "leaders as teachers" model and the EDP's redesign around the 6D framework are concrete illustrations of leadership-development approaches that combine formal curriculum with experiential, cross-domain learning — directly relevant to the session's leadership-development learning outcome.

Article Session 5

Is Yours a Learning Organization?

Harvard Business Review · David A. Garvin, Amy C. Edmondson, and Francesca Gino · March 2008 (Reprint R0803H)

Garvin, Edmondson, and Gino argue that decades of enthusiasm for "the learning organization" stalled because managers lacked concrete steps and assessment tools, and they respond with a granular diagnostic instrument — the Learning Organization Survey — built around three measurable building blocks: a supportive learning environment, concrete learning processes and practices, and leadership that reinforces learning. The article demonstrates the tool's diagnostic power through a case study of a pseudonymous European utility, "Eutilize," whose managers discovered their self-perceptions of strength did not match benchmarked reality.

Full digest: the three building blocks and their sub-components, the survey instrument and scoring method, the Eutilize case application, and the four concluding principles for managers

Why prior learning-organization efforts stalled

The authors trace the "learning organization" concept to its 1990s flourishing, catalyzed by Peter M. Senge's The Fifth Discipline, and identify three reasons progress stalled: early discussions were aspirational "paeans to a better world" rather than concrete prescriptions, offering no implementable sequence of steps; the concept targeted CEOs and senior executives rather than managers of the smaller departments and units where the critical organizational work actually happens; and standards and assessment tools were lacking, letting companies declare premature victory without rigorous comparison to peers. Their response is a "comprehensive, concrete survey instrument" measuring learning at the level of a department, office, project, or division.

Building Block 1 — A Supportive Learning Environment

This block has four distinguishing sub-components. Psychological safety means employees are not belittled or marginalized for disagreeing, asking naive questions, admitting mistakes, or voicing a minority view. Appreciation of differences means recognizing the value of competing functional outlooks and alternative worldviews, which increases energy and motivation and prevents drift. Openness to new ideas means encouraging risk-taking and exploration of the untested rather than merely correcting errors. Time for reflection means protecting space from deadline pressure so people can think analytically and creatively rather than being judged purely on hours worked and tasks completed. The authors illustrate this block with Julie Morath, COO of Children's Hospitals and Clinics of Minnesota, who instituted a policy of "blameless reporting," replacing threatening terms like "errors" and "investigations" with "accidents" and "analysis" to build a culture "of everyone working together to understand safety, identify risks, and report them without fear of blame" — yielding measurable reductions in preventable deaths and illnesses.

Building Block 2 — Concrete Learning Processes and Practices

This block treats learning as a set of concrete, widely distributed activities akin to logistics or order fulfillment, comprising five sub-components: experimentation (testing new products and services), information collection (intelligence gathering on competitors, customers, and trends), analysis (disciplined interpretation to solve problems), education and training, and information transfer (systematic sharing internally or externally). The paradigm example given is the U.S. Army's After Action Review (AAR), a systematic debriefing after every mission or project organized around four questions — What did we set out to do? What actually happened? Why did it happen? What do we do next time? — with results codified by the Center for Army Lessons Learned (CALL) and disseminated up, down, and laterally through the chain of command.

Building Block 3 — Leadership That Reinforces Learning

Leadership behavior — actively questioning, listening, and prompting dialogue and debate — signals that problem identification, knowledge transfer, and reflective post-audits matter, emboldening employees to offer new ideas. The authors cite Harvey Golub, former CEO of American Express, renowned for pushing managers to reason actively and think unconventionally; a subordinate observed he often "came at things from a different angle."

Harvey Golub, former CEO, American Express: "I am far less interested in people having the right answer than in their thinking about issues the right way. What criteria do they use? Why do they think the way they do? What alternatives have they considered? What premises do they have? What rocks are they standing on?"

The article notes the three blocks reinforce and overlap with one another in a "virtuous circle": leadership sustains supportive environments, which ease execution of concrete processes, which in turn give leaders more opportunities to model learning-reinforcing behavior.

The Learning Organization Survey instrument

The diagnostic tool asks respondents to rate, on a 1-to-7 scale, how accurately descriptive statements match their unit for building blocks 1 and 2 (e.g., "In this unit, it is easy to speak up about what is on your mind"; reverse-scored items such as "If you make a mistake in this unit, it is often held against you" are flagged with an asterisk), and on a 1-to-5 scale for leadership behaviors in building block 3 (e.g., "My managers invite input from others in discussions"; "My managers criticize views different from their own" is reverse-scored). Scores are scaled to 100 and benchmarked against baseline data gathered from Harvard Business School's eight-week general management program — first 100 executives in spring 2006 to validate the instrument, then 125 senior executives that autumn as the benchmark cohort — with results reported by quartile around a median for each sub-component and composite.

Case application: "Eutilize"

In summer 2006, 19 midlevel managers at a major European public utility the authors pseudonymously call Eutilize took the survey. Before seeing their scores, participants predicted average-or-better results, consistent with the company's stated goal of using knowledge and best-practice transfer as a competitive advantage. The actual results surprised them: on a 100-point scaled score, Eutilize managers rated themselves 68 on leadership (versus a median benchmark of 76), 58 on concrete learning processes (versus a median of 74), and 62 on supportive learning environment (versus a median of 71) — landing in the bottom quartile on openness to new ideas, experimentation, conflict and debate, and information transfer. Managers also discovered their mental models were inaccurate: many believed "analysis" was a strength, but had equated it narrowly with number-crunching rather than testing assumptions, debating productively, or seeking dissenting views. The authors emphasize the survey's value lay not in the raw scores but in prompting reflective, example-grounded discussion balanced between "advocacy and inquiry," and in pushing participants to identify specific corrective actions rather than lapse into unproductive complaint sessions.

Four concluding principles

The authors close with four practical insights drawn from testing the survey across dozens of organizations and four pilot firms of varying size, location, and mission. Leadership alone is insufficient: all four pilot firms scored higher on learning leadership than on concrete processes or supportive environment, showing that modeling behavior alone cannot substitute for explicit process and cultural interventions. Organizations are not monolithic: local cultures of learning vary widely across units (illustrated by differing rates of reported medical errors among nursing units within the same hospital), so a one-size-fits-all strategy is unlikely to succeed. Comparative performance is the critical scorecard: a high self-rated score conveys little, since most surveyed organizations independently claimed the same domains — "openness to new ideas" and "education and training" — as strengths; only benchmarked, relative scores are informative. Learning is multidimensional: because each building block itself has multiple independent sub-components responding to different levers, companies should not concentrate improvement efforts on a single lever (e.g., more reflection time) but should diagnose broadly before choosing where to intervene.

Practical implications

The three-building-block framework offers a direct lens for distinguishing skills-building (education and training, a discrete process sub-component) from broader organizational capability (the composite interaction of environment, process, and leadership). The Golub example is a compact illustration of leadership development as behavior modeling — teaching managers not answers but a way of reasoning — that resonates directly with this session's leadership-development learning outcome.

Article Session 5

It's Time for an L&D Revolution: The AI Era Arrives

The Josh Bersin Company · Josh Bersin and Jordan Hammerstad · 2025

Drawing on interviews with hundreds of L&D leaders and 40 chief learning officers, Josh Bersin argues that corporate learning is trapped in a legacy "publishing model" of courses, LMS platforms, and centralized content libraries that generates enormous waste and little measurable behavior change, and that generative AI now enables a fundamentally different "AI-first learning" paradigm in which content is generated dynamically, employees ask questions instead of taking courses, and L&D reinvents itself as a lean, decentralized "enablement" function rather than a training department.

Full digest: the legacy L&D crisis and its statistics, named vendor and company case studies, the AI-first learning architecture, the reinvented CLO role, and Bersin's four mandates for getting started

The scale and stagnation of corporate learning

Organizations now spend nearly $400 billion a year on employee training, professional development, and upskilling (citing Training Industry, Inc.'s 2025 State of the Corporate Training Market), driven by the World Economic Forum's estimate that 44 per cent of on-the-job skills will be disrupted by 2030. Yet Bersin's own Corporate Learning survey finds only 27 per cent of companies believe they are effectively building the skills and capabilities their business strategy requires, and only 15 per cent actively experiment with new learning technologies — even as adoption of adaptive learning (3%), personalized learning (8%), and VR/simulations (3%) remains low against blended learning (41%) and microlearning (26%). Only 15 per cent of organizations have L&D systems well integrated with other corporate systems, only 12 per cent offer career pathways, and only 16 per cent have programs to advance skills within employees' current jobs. The report traces L&D's evolution across four eras — corporate universities modeled on GE's Crotonville in the 1990s, the rise of global chief learning officers (CLOs) managing corporate LMSs in the 2000s, integration under HR talent management around 2010, and the present shift toward an AI-driven "enablement" model.

Legacy technology, content, and operating models

Legacy Learning Management Systems (LMSs), designed in the late 1990s primarily as compliance-tracking systems, cannot natively interpret their own content and require manual tagging; a banking client is cited as requiring employees to click at least six times through tens of thousands of items just to start a course. Cloud software firm Blackbaud is cited as having juggled seven disparate LMSs before consolidating, causing operational inefficiency and a diminished learner experience. Vendors named as having innovated within the legacy paradigm include Degreed, Microsoft Viva Learning, 360Learning, Udemy, LinkedIn, LearnUpon, and Docebo. On the content side, providers such as Skillsoft, Khan Academy, Udemy, Coursera, Pluralsight, LinkedIn, and Harvard Publishing continue to build long-form "courses" that are hard to update; Bersin's client Rolls-Royce reportedly noted that mastering maintenance of a single jet engine would take 500 years of learning to complete under the traditional model. Operationally, interviews with 40 chief learning officers surfaced consistent complaints about complex, hard-to-navigate platforms and slow time to market; the report finds only 10 per cent of companies believe their L&D team is proficient with gen-AI tools, only 4 per cent have redesigned L&D processes around AI, and only 8 per cent use AI to automate administrative tasks. A global financial services client is cited as employing 110 instructional designers. Further research findings: only 33 per cent of organizations believe their L&D strategy aligns with business strategy, only 14 per cent say HR leadership is committed to future-proofing L&D team skills, and only 16 per cent focus on developing L&D professionals' consulting capabilities.

Case in point: Galileo Learn (Josh Bersin Company / Sana)

Galileo Learn, the successor to the Josh Bersin Academy (JBA) and developed in partnership with AI vendor Sana, delivers AI-driven simulations and hyperpersonalized learning journeys for HR professionals, with conversational AI interfaces letting users explore content directly relevant to their needs. Amy Farner, Executive Vice President of Product at The Josh Bersin Company, is quoted on the transition:

Amy Farner, EVP of Product, The Josh Bersin Company: "The old ways of working will not survive the AI revolution. If organizations are not willing to radically transform the ways they create content and the modalities of learning that are available, they risk becoming irrelevant."

Case in point: Moderna

Biotechnology company Moderna merged its digital technology and HR functions to accelerate AI-enabled learning. Molly Nagler, Head of Learning at Moderna, states that AI is Moderna's "force multiplier." Moderna's L&D stack includes Arist (for targeted microlearning delivered through Microsoft Teams) and Synthesia (for rapid AI-avatar video production), plus custom-built GPTs providing on-demand learner support.

Molly Nagler, Head of Learning, Moderna: "AI is our force multiplier—it helps us scale learning with precision and connect people to impact faster."
Greg Dracos, Senior Director of Learner Experience & Innovation, Moderna: "We're building a powerful AI-enabled learning stack that reduces development time, automates support, and brings high-quality learning into the flow of work."

In one instance, Moderna used AI to review training requirements against roles and streamlined mandatory training for a 200-person employee group, saving thousands of full-time-equivalent (FTE) hours of duplicative training.

The cost of waste in legacy L&D

Organizations spend an average of $1,100 per employee annually on L&D, with more than 40 per cent of that budget going to external platforms, services, and content — much of it duplicative. The report cites Hermann Ebbinghaus's 1913 research finding that 90 per cent of learning is forgotten within a week without reinforcement; the 1995 Robinson and Robinson study finding that most corporate training does not produce behavior change; and a 2016 Harvard Business Review article by Michael Beer, Magnus Finnström, and Derek Schrader ("Why Leadership Training Fails—and What to Do About It") finding "little evidence of impact" from corporate training. Training magazine's 2024 Training Industry Report finds 30 to 40 per cent of L&D spending goes to instructional design, of which Bersin estimates nearly half could be eliminated or reallocated through AI-driven platforms. Global event-management firm mci group, through its MCI Institute Centre of Excellence, is cited as having partnered with Sana to decentralize its learning model and shift its lean L&D team into a consulting role generating revenue from external clients.

What AI-first learning means

"AI-first learning" replaces or heavily augments the LMS with a dynamic content-generation platform capable of producing tutors, simulations, and course assistants and updating content in minutes, letting employees "ask a question" instead of taking a course. The report contrasts a legacy client/server architecture (content in a central LMS, thin-client user access) with an emerging AI-native peer-to-peer architecture, in which every employee has a personal large language model (LLM) on their device communicating with an AI server that dynamically generates or curates content, enabling local translation, localization, and personalization — illustrated by a hypothetical global distribution company training truck drivers in real time and a call-center travel company using local LLMs to record conversations and deliver personalized coaching. Wolters Kluwer is cited using Arist to build and deploy Microsoft Teams-embedded microlearning within minutes, including a rapid global rollout of generative-AI training. At cloud data platform Databricks, "learning architects" — led by Rochana Golani, VP of Learning and Enablement, who reports to a cofounder overseeing field engineering — design entire learning experiences rather than traditional curricula. Creative-services firm Superside democratized self-authoring tools built on Sana, letting employees build their own courses; after AI training, Superside's creative teams reduced design creation time by up to 80 per cent, a gain passed on to clients as reduced billable creative hours.

The reinvented CLO and organizational model

The chief learning officer (CLO) title dates to 1994, created under Jack Welch at GE to help transform the company's management practices; the role's influence subsequently diminished as CLOs were folded several levels below the CHRO within HR. Bersin argues AI-first learning is elevating the CLO again into a business-aligned, tech-enabled leader. The report proposes a three-tier AI-first operating model: Tier 1 (Enterprise Team), a lean group aligning AI tools, learning technology, and IT infrastructure under the CHRO/CLO and curating enterprise-wide content; Tier 2 (Functional and Regional), business leaders and learning professionals maintaining context-specific content; and Tier 3 (Individuals and Teams), employees who dynamically publish hyperpersonalized content and drive peer-to-peer enablement.

Four mandates for getting started

Bersin lays out four steps for organizations pursuing this shift. First, modernize the learning tech stack with AI-native platforms (such as Sana) that surpass legacy LMS/LXP/authoring-tool capability. Second, upskill and transform the L&D team itself: citing the company's HR Capability Project, only 7 per cent of L&D professionals feel expert in AI tools and platforms, only 8 per cent in content/product management, only 15 per cent in design thinking, only 14 per cent in organizational design, and only 16 per cent have deep understanding of global culture — against stronger self-rated capability in coaching (49%), needs analysis/performance consulting (43%), onboarding program development (42%), developing managers and leaders (41%), and linking competencies to learning paths (31%). Third, position corporate learning as enablement, not training, spanning information access, process guidance, skills development, and certified capabilities rather than courses alone. Fourth, build a lean, AI-powered operating model using the three-tier structure above. A construction-management software company is cited as an exemplar of tying learning to business strategy, defining skills across four levels — beginner, approaching, proficient, advanced — and using AI platform Uplimit to deliver single-skill courses precisely mapped to skill-gap closure.

Conclusion and key takeaways

Bersin's key takeaways: despite massive investment, most companies remain stuck with outdated learning models that fail to deliver business impact; learning must become dynamic, personalized, and enablement-focused; CLOs must reclaim their position as enterprise leaders of workforce enablement, not just learning; and AI unlocks faster content creation, lower costs, and reduced operational inefficiency while enabling a broader strategic L&D role.

Practical implications

The report's four-level skills framework (beginner/approaching/proficient/advanced) and its distinction between "information," "process," "skills," and "capabilities" as tiers of enablement content directly extend this session's skills-competencies-capabilities vocabulary into an AI-native delivery context. Its account of the CLO's rise, fall, and re-elevation is a live case of how leadership-development ownership and organizational status shift with technological and strategic context.
